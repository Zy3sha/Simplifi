A rash appears after breakfast. Your baby coughs once. There is yoghurt, egg and toast on the tray. In a frightening moment, parents are expected to decide what matters and remember every detail.

**The short answer:** breathing difficulty, swelling of the mouth or throat, unusual floppiness, collapse or rapidly worsening symptoms can mean anaphylaxis — call **999** now and say you think it is an allergic reaction. If your child has a prescribed adrenaline auto-injector, follow their allergy plan and use it as instructed. For a milder suspected reaction, stop the food, keep your baby with you, seek medical advice and do not offer the suspected food again unless a qualified clinician tells you how.

The record comes second. Safety comes first.

## What to do if your baby reacts to food

Use the most serious symptom, not the size of the rash, to decide what happens next.

| What you notice | What to do |
|---|---|
| Swelling of the lips, mouth, throat or tongue; difficult or very fast breathing; wheeze, choking or gasping; blue, grey or very pale colour; sudden drowsiness, floppiness, confusion or collapse | Call **999 immediately** and say you suspect anaphylaxis. Follow the child's allergy action plan and use prescribed adrenaline as directed. Do not wait to create a log. |
| A milder possible reaction such as a small itchy rash, swollen lips or face, vomiting, tummy symptoms, watery eyes, cough or worsening eczema, while your baby is otherwise breathing and responding normally | Stop the food, stay with your baby and seek medical advice. If you are unsure how urgent it is, use **NHS 111**; call 999 if symptoms become severe or rapidly worsen. |
| Gagging while learning to manage texture — noisy coughing or retching, often with the tongue pushed forward and normal colour | Stay calm and supervise closely. Do not put fingers into the mouth blindly. Learn the separate signs of [gagging versus choking](/blog/gagging-vs-choking-baby-weaning.html). |

A severe allergic reaction can develop quickly. Do not delay an emergency call while taking photographs, searching symptoms or opening an app.

## What can a food-allergy reaction look like?

NHS guidance says immediate reactions usually begin within minutes and can take up to two hours to appear. Some allergies, including some cows' milk allergy, can produce delayed symptoms over as long as three days.

Possible symptoms include:

- swollen lips or face
- red, itchy or watery eyes
- a red, raised or itchy rash
- coughing, wheezing or breathing difficulty
- feeling or being sick
- tummy pain, diarrhoea or constipation
- a runny or blocked nose
- worsening eczema or asthma symptoms

One sign alone cannot confirm the cause. Tomato or strawberry touching sensitive skin can leave local redness; a baby can vomit because of illness; gagging is common during texture learning. Equally, a food that has been eaten safely before is not automatically ruled out — allergies can appear after previous uneventful exposures.

That uncertainty is why a factual timeline is more useful than writing “definitely allergic”.

## The six details worth recording

Once your baby is safe and you have followed medical advice, make a short record:

1. **Exact food and ingredients.** “Porridge with cows' milk and smooth peanut butter” is more useful than “breakfast”.
2. **Preparation.** Note the brand, recipe and whether egg was baked, scrambled, hard-boiled or runny, for example.
3. **Time and amount.** Record when eating began, roughly how much was swallowed and when the first symptom appeared.
4. **What you actually saw.** Write “three raised red patches on the chest” or “vomited twice”, not a diagnosis.
5. **Other things happening that day.** Include fever, illness, vaccination and medicines, without assuming they caused the response.
6. **What happened next.** Record who you contacted, treatment given on professional advice and when symptoms changed.

If it is safe, photograph a visible skin change in good light. For packaged food, keep the wrapper or photograph the full ingredients list, allergen statement, brand and batch code. A clinician may also want to know which parts of the meal your baby has eaten before without a problem.

## Do not repeat the food as a home “test”

After a suspected reaction, stop offering the food and speak to your GP, health visitor or allergy team before trying it again. Do not give a little more to see whether it happens twice.

A clinician may decide that a supervised challenge, allergy testing or a structured reintroduction is appropriate. That decision depends on the symptoms, timing, food, preparation and your baby's medical history. It is not something a tracking app can determine.

Avoid removing whole food groups for weeks without professional support. Excluding milk, egg or wheat can affect nutrition, and symptoms attributed to one ingredient may have another explanation. Take the record to a professional and let it support a proper assessment.

## Reaction, gagging, choking or refusal?

These moments need different responses, so keep their records separate.

| Event | Typical clue | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Possible allergy | Itchy rash, swelling, vomiting, cough, wheeze, breathing change or symptoms away from the mouth | Stop the food and follow medical or emergency advice. |
| Gagging | Usually noisy; baby may cough, retch, go red and push the tongue forward | A protective reflex while learning texture, though supervision is essential. |
| Choking | Baby may be quiet, unable to breathe or cough effectively, or change colour | An emergency requiring age-appropriate choking first aid and 999 when indicated. |
| Refusal | Turns away, closes mouth, pushes food away, with no physical symptoms | Normal communication, not evidence of allergy. End the pressure and try another day unless there was a suspected reaction. |

## A calmer setup for the next new allergen

When your baby is well and you are following any individual clinical advice:

- introduce a food that can trigger allergy one at a time and in a small amount
- use a safe texture and sit your baby upright
- choose a time when you can observe rather than rushing out
- write the specific ingredients, not just the meal name
- keep tolerated foods in the usual varied diet
- make sure every caregiver knows the child's allergy plan, if they have one

If your baby has diagnosed food allergy or eczema, or there is a family history of food allergy, eczema, asthma or hay fever, speak to your GP or health visitor before introductions. ## Frequently asked questions

### Can a baby react to a food they have eaten before?

Yes. Previous tolerance is useful context but does not rule out a later reaction. Stop the suspected food and seek advice rather than testing it again yourself.

### Is redness around the mouth always an allergy?

No. Food contact can irritate sensitive skin, especially with acidic foods, and skin redness alone cannot diagnose allergy. Record where it appeared, whether it was itchy or raised, and whether there were symptoms elsewhere. Seek medical advice if you suspect a reaction.

### Should I give an antihistamine?

Follow your child's written allergy plan or advice from a qualified clinician. Antihistamines do not replace adrenaline for anaphylaxis and should never delay calling 999.

### How long should I watch after a new food?

Immediate food-allergy symptoms commonly appear within minutes and can take up to two hours, while some reactions are delayed. Introduce one new allergenic food at a time when your baby is well and you can observe them, then keep a factual record of any later symptoms.

### Can OBubba tell me which food caused the reaction?

No. OBubba can organise foods, detected allergen groups, timing and parent-labelled responses, and it can highlight cautious patterns. Only a qualified healthcare professional can assess and diagnose food allergy.
