You bought the highchair, steamed the broccoli and checked whether your baby is ready for solids. Then a quieter thought appears: *What would I do if they choked?*

You do not need to become fearless before the first meal. You do need a plan you have practised with your hands, not just a video you once watched at 1am.

Before weaning, the most valuable preparation is to take a practical baby-and-child first-aid course that covers choking and infant CPR. Add safer food preparation, upright supervised meals, emergency numbers and a brief reference that every regular carer can find. An article or app can refresh memory; neither can reproduce feedback from an instructor or the physical feel of positioning a training manikin.

This UK guide explains what to learn, how to reduce risk and what an emergency sequence is trying to achieve. It is **not** a substitute for accredited training. In a real emergency, call 999 and follow the call handler's instructions.

## The before-weaning first-aid checklist

Try to complete these before, or soon after, the first tastes begin:

1. **Book a practical baby-and-child first-aid course.** Choose one that lets you practise choking manoeuvres and CPR on an infant manikin.
2. **Ask every regular carer to learn.** A partner, grandparent, nanny or nursery worker may be the adult at the meal.
3. **Learn the difference between gagging and choking.** Gagging is noisy and protective; severe choking may be quiet because air is not moving.
4. **Practise infant choking first aid.** For a baby under one, this uses back blows and chest thrusts, not abdominal thrusts.
5. **Practise infant CPR.** Learn how to assess response and breathing, call 999, give rescue breaths and deliver compressions.
6. **Prepare food by size, shape and texture.** Prevention matters at every meal, even after a course.
7. **Set up the highchair correctly.** Baby should be alert, securely strapped and sitting upright.
8. **Keep your phone available, but not distracting you.** Know your address and how to put an emergency call on speaker.
9. **Save the non-emergency route too.** NHS 111 can advise when the situation is urgent but not immediately life-threatening.
10. **Refresh the skill.** Revisit guidance and retrain periodically rather than assuming one class lasts forever.

The purpose is calm readiness, not expecting disaster. Choking is uncommon, but recognising it quickly and acting decisively matters.

## Gagging and choking are not the same

Gagging is a normal protective reflex as babies learn to move food around their mouth. It can look dramatic. A gagging baby may cough, retch, make noise, push their tongue forward, water at the eyes or vomit. Their skin may redden, although colour changes can be less obvious on some skin tones.

Choking means the airway is blocked. In severe choking, a baby may be unable to cry, make noise, cough effectively or breathe. Colour can change; on brown or black skin, blue or grey colour may be easier to see on the gums, inside the lips or fingernails.

The [NHS Best Start in Life guide to gagging and choking](https://www.nhs.uk/best-start-in-life/baby/weaning/safe-weaning/choking-and-gagging-on-food/) describes gagging as loud and choking as quiet. That sound difference is a useful first clue, not a reason to stand back if breathing is ineffective.

When baby is gagging and breathing, stay calm and let the protective reflex work. Do not put a finger blindly into their mouth, pat their back or try to pull food out when you cannot clearly see and safely grasp it; you could push it further back. Our detailed [gagging-versus-choking guide](/blog/gagging-vs-choking-baby-weaning.html) includes a comparison parents can review before the meal.

When baby cannot breathe properly, cough effectively, cry or make noise, treat it as choking and act immediately.

## What infant choking first aid involves

The exact positioning and force are why hands-on teaching matters. The sequence below is a memory aid based on current UK guidance, not training.

For a conscious baby under one with severe choking:

1. **Shout for help and remove baby from the highchair.** If another person is present, have them call 999 while you act.
2. **Give up to five back blows.** Support baby's head and neck, position them face-down with the head lower than the body, and give sharp blows between the shoulder blades. Check whether the obstruction has cleared after each blow.
3. **If it has not cleared, give up to five chest thrusts.** Turn baby face-up while supporting the head. An instructor should teach you the correct position and technique.
4. **Call 999 if the blockage has not cleared.** Put the phone on speaker and continue to follow the emergency call handler.
5. **Alternate up to five back blows and five chest thrusts** until the obstruction clears or baby becomes unresponsive.
6. **If baby becomes unresponsive, start infant CPR.** Begin with five rescue breaths, as current UK paediatric guidance describes, and follow the call handler.

The [NHS choking guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/first-aid-and-safety/first-aid/how-to-stop-a-child-from-choking/) describes up to five back blows followed by up to five chest thrusts for a baby under one, calling 999 if the blockage remains, and continuing the cycle until help arrives. If the baby becomes unconscious, call 999 on speaker and start CPR.

Important boundaries:

- Do **not** use abdominal thrusts on a baby under one; chest thrusts are used instead.
- Do **not** hold a baby upside down by the feet.
- Do **not** perform a blind finger sweep. Remove an object only if it is clearly visible and can be safely reached.
- Stop choking manoeuvres if the obstruction clears and baby is breathing effectively.
- A baby who received chest thrusts or compressions should be assessed by a healthcare professional because injury or complications can occur.

Suction airway-clearance devices are not a replacement for standard first aid. Current Resuscitation Council UK guidance says there is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against these devices. Do not let a gadget delay established actions or a 999 call.

## What to know about infant CPR

CPR is for a baby who is unresponsive and not breathing normally, not for a baby who is noisily gagging or coughing effectively.

A practical course should teach you to:

- check for danger and response
- open an infant's airway in a neutral position
- assess normal breathing for no more than ten seconds
- call 999 on speaker
- give five initial rescue breaths, using just enough air to make the chest rise
- position your hands correctly for infant chest compressions
- compress around one-third of the chest depth at the taught rate
- continue cycles of 30 compressions and two breaths if you are an untrained lone rescuer following public guidance
- continue until signs of life return or emergency professionals take over

Current [Resuscitation Council UK public CPR guidance](https://www.resus.org.uk/public-resource/how-do-cpr) teaches the two-thumb encircling technique for infant compressions. An instructor can correct placement, depth, seal and pace on a manikin, feedback a written checklist cannot provide.

If you freeze in a real emergency, call 999, put the phone on speaker and say clearly that a baby is unresponsive or not breathing normally. The call handler will guide you. Doing imperfect CPR is better than doing nothing because you are trying to recall an illustration exactly.

## Prevent choking at the meal

First aid is the back-up. The daily safety work is how baby sits, how food is prepared and whether an attentive adult stays close.

### Seat baby upright and alert

Use a stable highchair with its harness securely fitted. Baby should sit safely upright, not reclined, lying down or wandering with food. Wait if they are very sleepy or upset. Stay within reach for the entire meal; “I can see them from the sink” is not the same as supervision.

Read our [three signs of readiness for solids](/blog/is-my-baby-ready-for-solids.html) before treating age alone as permission to begin.

### Change risky shapes and textures

The [NHS food-preparation guide](https://www.nhs.uk/best-start-in-life/baby/weaning/safe-weaning/preparing-food-safely/) advises thinking about size, shape and texture. In practice:

- cook firm vegetables and fruit until soft enough for the stage
- cut small round fruit such as grapes and cherry tomatoes lengthways into quarters
- remove pips, stones, bones, tough skin and gristle
- cut sausages lengthways into thin strips rather than coin-shaped rounds
- grate or cut cheese into short narrow strips
- lightly toast white bread, which can otherwise form a doughy ball
- spread nut butter thinly or stir it into food; do not offer a spoonful by itself
- do not give whole nuts or popcorn to children under five
- keep hard sweets, marshmallows, raw jelly cubes and other hard, sticky choking hazards away

Use the finger-and-thumb squash test as one practical clue for soft foods, but still follow current serving guidance for the specific food and your child's developmental stage.

### Keep small objects out of reach

Food is not the only risk. Coins, beads, pen lids, marbles, deflated balloons and parts from an older child's toys can fit into a baby's mouth. Button batteries are especially dangerous if swallowed and need immediate emergency assessment. Get down to floor level periodically and look at the room from the baby's new crawling perspective.

### Do not rely on one feeding style as protection

Choking risk is managed through readiness, upright positioning, supervision and suitable food preparation, not by declaring purées or baby-led weaning automatically safe. Babies can choke on food or small objects. Whichever approach you use, progress texture responsively and prepare each food appropriately. See our balanced guide to [baby-led weaning versus spoon-feeding](/blog/baby-led-weaning-vs-spoon-feeding.html).

## Make the environment emergency-ready

Preparation should reduce decisions when adrenaline is high.

### Know where you are

At home, write the address and postcode where any regular carer can find it. At a holiday cottage or family visit, note the address on arrival. An emergency operator needs a location; “the barn after the lane” costs time.

### Keep the phone reachable

Have a charged phone close enough to use, but keep it out of baby's reach and do not let it pull your attention from eating. Learn how to activate speakerphone. If you use Wi-Fi calling in an area with poor signal, understand how emergency calling works on your device and do not assume a voice assistant will replace a clear call.

### Give carers a two-minute safety handover

Before somebody else serves a meal, confirm:

- baby must be upright, harnessed and watched continuously
- what food is being offered and how it has been prepared
- known allergies and where prescribed emergency medicine is kept
- the home address, emergency number and phone location
- when and how to call you, without delaying 999
- that they know infant choking first aid

If they do not know what to do in an emergency, that is information to act on before leaving them to supervise solids.

### Keep health information accurate

Store prescribed allergy action plans and medicines exactly as clinicians advise. Check expiry dates. Never use an app's medicine log as permission to administer a dose. For safe shared recording, see our [baby medicine handover guide](/blog/baby-medicine-log-prevent-double-dosing.html).

## Four other first-aid skills worth learning

A good baby-and-child course goes beyond choking.

### Burns and scalds

Hot drinks, kettles, pans and bath water are common risks. If a child is burned, remove nearby clothing or jewellery unless it is stuck, then cool the area under cool running water for 20 to 30 minutes. Keep the rest of the child warm. Do not apply ice, butter, toothpaste, cream, oil or gel. After cooling, lay cling film over the burn rather than wrapping it tightly.

The [NHS accident guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/first-aid-and-safety/first-aid/what-to-do-if-your-child-has-an-accident/) gives those cooling and covering steps. Seek medical advice for a burn to a baby or young child; call 999 for a serious injury while continuing cooling when practical.

### Fever and serious illness

A fever is 38°C or more. The [NHS fever guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/fever-in-children/) says to call NHS 111 or a GP urgently for a baby under three months with 38°C or higher, or a baby aged three to six months with 39°C or higher. Seek help for other concerning signs regardless of the number.

Call 999 for emergency signs including a child who stops breathing, will not wake, has a first febrile seizure, has a severe allergic reaction, or has a red or purple rash that does not fade under a glass. The [current NHS urgent-help guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/health/when-to-get-urgent-medical-help-for-babies-and-children-under-5/) also flags breathing difficulty, being difficult to wake, a weak, high-pitched or continuous cry, and no wet nappies for 12 hours.

### Seizures

If a child has a seizure, note the time, protect their head and move dangerous objects away. Do not restrain them or put anything in their mouth. The [NHS febrile-seizure guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/febrile-seizures/) says to call 999 for a first seizure, one lasting more than five minutes, breathing difficulty, one-sided movements, more than one seizure in 24 hours, or unusual sleepiness lasting more than an hour afterwards. If they are still seizing or unconscious, do not drive them to A&E; call an ambulance.

### Severe allergic reaction

Learn the signs in advance, particularly when introducing allergens. Sudden breathing difficulty, swelling of the mouth, throat or tongue, collapse, floppiness or reduced responsiveness can indicate anaphylaxis. Call 999 immediately and say “anaphylaxis.” Follow the child's allergy action plan and use prescribed adrenaline exactly as trained. Do not wait for a rash; severe reactions do not always include one.

Our [baby-allergen introduction guide](/blog/how-to-introduce-baby-allergens-without-losing-track.html) explains how to introduce and record one allergen clearly without confusing normal mess with a reaction.

## How to choose a first-aid course

Look for baby-and-child-specific training delivered by a recognised provider or suitably qualified instructor. Before booking, ask:

- Does it cover infants under one as well as older children?
- Will every participant practise on a manikin?
- Does it follow current UK Resuscitation Council guidance?
- Does it cover choking, CPR, recovery position and emergency escalation?
- Can partners, grandparents or other carers attend?
- How long is the practical session, and when is refresher training advised?

An online module can be useful preparation or revision. It cannot tell you whether your back blow is effective, your airway position is neutral or your compression technique needs adjusting. If access or cost is difficult, ask your health visitor, children's centre, local council, nursery or community organisation about subsidised sessions.

After training, practise the spoken sequence occasionally with an empty highchair and a training prop only: “Shout. Out of chair. Back blows. Chest thrusts. 999. CPR if unresponsive.” Never practise choking manoeuvres or compressions on a healthy baby.

## What to do this week

Do not try to memorise an entire emergency manual tonight. Take four concrete actions:

1. Book a practical infant first-aid course for every regular carer who can attend.
2. Read the current NHS choking-prevention and food-preparation pages.
3. Check that the highchair is stable, harnessed and upright.
4. Put your address, emergency number and allergy plan where carers can find them.

Then start solids when your baby is ready, with suitable food and your attention at the table. Prepared does not mean expecting the worst. It means the joyful mess of learning to eat is supported by skills you hope never to need.
