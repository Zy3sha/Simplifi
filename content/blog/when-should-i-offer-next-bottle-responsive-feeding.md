The last bottle was two hours ago. Your baby is chewing a fist, turning their head and becoming restless. Is it too soon to feed again, or are you meant to wait for the “right” interval?

**The short answer:** offer a bottle when your baby shows early hunger cues, not when a timer grants permission. The NHS recommends responsive or on-demand feeding rather than a strict schedule. A recent pattern can help you prepare, share care and notice an unusual gap, but it should never be used to delay a hungry baby or make them finish a predicted amount.

The clock is context. Your baby decides whether to start, pause and stop.

## Early hunger cues to watch for

Babies are usually easier to feed before they become upset. Early hunger cues can include:

- stirring, wriggling or becoming more alert
- bringing hands or fingers to the mouth
- opening and closing the mouth
- turning the head and rooting for the teat
- sucking movements or searching with the eyes

Crying is a late hunger cue, although babies cry for many reasons. If your baby fed very recently, first check whether they need a cuddle, change, wind, cooler clothing or help with discomfort. If the hunger cues continue, offer the feed.

Responsive feeding is not the same as guessing every cry is hunger. It means noticing the whole baby rather than enforcing a fixed interval.

## There is no universal bottle interval

The NHS says babies vary in how often they want to feed and how much they take. They often feed little and often, particularly in the early months and during growth spurts.

Some babies gradually develop a recognisable rhythm. That rhythm can help a caregiver know when to sterilise equipment or prepare to feed, but it is not a contract. A baby may ask sooner after a smaller feed, wake later after a long nap or take several close feeds during a growth spurt.

Do not stretch feeds to make a chart look tidy. For newborns and young babies, responsive feeding and any individual plan from the midwife, neonatal team or health visitor take priority.

## A big bottle does not buy a longer gap

NHS guidance is explicit: a large feed does not mean a baby will go longer before the next one. Trying to “fill them up” can override fullness cues and cause distress.

Instead:

1. Hold your baby close in a semi-upright position with their head supported.
2. Brush the teat gently over the top lip and let your baby draw it in.
3. Keep the bottle almost horizontal and only slightly tipped so the flow is manageable.
4. Let your baby pause, breathe and restart.
5. Stop when they show they have had enough, even if milk remains.

Never prop a bottle or leave a baby feeding alone. Throw away milk left after the feed according to current NHS preparation guidance; do not save a partly used bottle for the next feed.

## How to recognise “I need a break” or “I am full”

Your baby may:

- slow or stop sucking
- let milk spill from the mouth
- turn away or push the teat out
- splay fingers or toes
- become relaxed or sleepy
- lose interest in restarting after a pause

Pause the flow and see what your baby communicates. Wind them if needed, then offer again only if they show continued hunger. Never force a baby to empty a bottle.

## What the clock is useful for

Timing still has practical value. A feed log can help answer:

- When was the last bottle?
- How much was offered and how much was taken?
- Was it expressed breast milk or formula?
- Is this gap very different from the baby's recent pattern?
- Did feeds cluster after poor daytime intake?
- Are two caregivers about to offer the same feed?
- Is the baby feeding less often alongside fewer wet nappies or illness?

Record the feed that happened, not the amount you hoped would happen. If 180ml was offered and 110ml was taken, the useful intake entry is 110ml.

## A practical decision guide

| Situation | Helpful response |
|---|---|
| Early hunger cues, regardless of the predicted time | Offer a responsive feed. The cue outranks the estimate. |
| Predicted feed time is approaching but baby is calm and shows no cues | Prepare if convenient, but do not coax or force a feed solely because the clock says so. |
| Baby stops well before the expected amount | Pause and respect fullness cues. Do not pressure them to finish. |
| Baby still cues after the expected amount | Continue responsively. A typical range is not an individual ceiling; seek advice if intake repeatedly worries you. |
| Very young baby, premature baby or a clinical feeding plan is in place | Follow the plan from the maternity, neonatal, dietetic or paediatric team rather than an app prediction. |
| Markedly reduced feeding, fewer wet nappies, unusual sleepiness or illness | Seek medical advice; do not wait for the next scheduled feed. |

## Breastfeeding, combination feeding and bottles need different maths

A breastfeeding session cannot be converted reliably into millilitres from duration alone. A ten-minute feed can be effective for one baby and incomplete for another. Pump output is milk expressed, not proof of what the baby drank at the breast.

For combination feeding, bottle millilitres are only the measured part of the day. A safe tracker should not label total intake “low” because it cannot see milk transferred at the breast.

If you are using formula top-ups while protecting breast-milk supply, ask your midwife, health visitor or infant-feeding specialist for an individual plan. Adding formula feeds can affect supply, so timing and expressing advice depend on your goal and circumstances.

## What counts as enough milk?

One bottle is not the best measure. NHS guidance points parents toward the wider picture, including growth and wet and dirty nappies. From a few days after birth, around six wet nappies a day is a common reassuring sign, although individual clinical advice matters.

Speak to your midwife or health visitor if you are worried about weight gain, feeding frequency, pain, repeated vomiting, difficulty coordinating sucking and breathing, or whether your baby is getting enough milk.

Get medical help urgently if your baby is hard to wake, has difficulty breathing, is blue, grey or unusually pale, feeds markedly less than normal, has much drier nappies or otherwise seems seriously unwell.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I wake my baby for a bottle?

It depends on age, growth, prematurity and clinical advice. Newborns or babies with weight-gain concerns may need scheduled waking. Follow the plan from your midwife, neonatal team or health visitor rather than a general app estimate.

### Is two hours too soon for another bottle?

Not automatically. Babies often feed little and often, and needs change during growth spurts. Look for genuine hunger cues and feed responsively.

### Should I make the next bottle larger after a short feed?

Prepare feeds safely and follow your baby's cues. An earlier small feed does not obligate your baby to take a larger bottle later, and a big feed will not necessarily create a longer gap.

### Can I use the predicted amount as a minimum?

No. It is neither a target to finish nor a minimum. Stop when your baby shows fullness cues, even if milk remains.
