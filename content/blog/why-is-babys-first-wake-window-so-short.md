Your baby wakes at 6:42am, feeds, smiles for a few minutes and starts rubbing their eyes by 7:15. They are asleep again at 7:28—long before the wake-window chart says Nap 1 should happen.

Why is the first wake window so short?

**Often, it is either your baby’s naturally shortest awake stretch, recovery after a broken night, or not a true wake window at all.** A brief calm wake followed by another substantial sleep may be the tail end of night sleep—a morning resettle—rather than Nap 1.

Do not stretch an exhausted baby just to make the timetable look right. First decide whether the day genuinely started. Then compare the same first wake window across several ordinary mornings and adjust in small steps only if the pattern is causing a problem.

> **The first question is not “How long should they stay awake?” It is “Were they fully awake for the day?”**

Wake windows are planning tools, not NHS targets or medical milestones. Babies vary, and newborn sleep is especially changeable. This guide is for a baby who is breathing normally, feeding as expected and otherwise seems well.

## The 60-second morning check

When your baby stirs near morning:

1. **Look at their state.** Are their eyes wide and focused, or fluttering and heavy? Is their body active or still floppy and drowsy?
2. **Keep the transition quiet.** Avoid bright lights, play and a full “good morning” production until you know they are awake.
3. **Respond to needs.** Feed responsively and change a nappy when needed. Do not withhold care to preserve a sleep label.
4. **See what happens naturally.** Do they become alert after care, or remain sleepy and drift back off?
5. **Check the night context.** An early start, long wake, illness, travel or unusually fragmented night can shorten the next comfortable awake stretch.

The NHS describes a drowsy or dozing baby as having heavy or fluttering eyelids, few movements and the ability to fall asleep again. A quiet, alert baby is wide-eyed, able to focus and ready to interact. That distinction is often more useful than the time on the clock.

## Is it Nap 1 or the end of night sleep?

No single sign decides. Use the whole sequence.

| What happened | More like a morning resettle | More like a true short first wake window |
|---|---|---|
| state on waking | drowsy, calm, heavy eyelids, little interest in play | clearly alert, engaged, looking around or ready to interact |
| time awake | often only 10–60 minutes | can still be short, but includes a recognisable awake period |
| what happened between sleeps | little beyond quiet reassurance; perhaps a sleepy feed | feed, nappy change, light, conversation or activity that genuinely started the day |
| return to sleep | drifts back easily with ordinary night-time support | needs the usual nap routine or shows clear tired cues after being alert |
| next sleep | feels like another block of night and may be substantial | behaves like Nap 1 within the daytime rhythm |
| what to log today | consider moving the final morning wake to the end of this sleep | keep the original wake and log this as Nap 1 |

If you are unsure, there is no dangerous bookkeeping error to avoid. Keep it as Nap 1, add a short note and watch what repeats. Correcting a label later is better than forcing the baby to stay awake so the data looks tidy.

## Why the first wake window can genuinely be shortest

### 1. The night did not fully restore them

A baby can spend many hours in the cot and still have a fragmented night. Repeated feeds, wakes, discomfort, an early start or a long split night may leave less comfortable awake stamina in the morning.

Look for the pair:

- shorter first awake stretch after the more broken nights
- a more typical first wake window after better-consolidated nights

If that relationship repeats, the short morning is useful feedback—not a behaviour to push through.

### 2. The day-start anchor is wrong

If a sleepy stir is logged as “morning wake”, every prediction after it starts too early. The app may then call a 7:30 sleep Nap 1 even though the baby’s biological day did not begin until 8:30.

This is common when a baby:

- wakes quietly before the household’s usual start
- feeds with lights low
- barely interacts
- returns to sleep within an hour
- wakes brighter and more sociable after the second sleep

Treating the second wake as the final morning wake can make the rest of the day suddenly make sense.

### 3. The baby is still very young

Newborns sleep in short bursts and do not arrive with a mature day-night rhythm. The NHS says babies have their own patterns and some sleep in long periods while others sleep in short bursts.

For a young baby, feeding, safe sleep and responsive care matter more than stretching a wake window. Follow any plan from your midwife, neonatal team or paediatrician, including instructions to wake for feeds.

### 4. Their current rhythm really begins slowly

Some babies are simply ready for their first nap sooner than later naps. A calm baby who feeds well, has a comfortable short first window, takes a restorative nap and manages the rest of the day does not need fixing because an age chart shows a larger number.

### 5. They are in a transition

Awake stamina changes gradually. The first window may lengthen before other windows, or temporarily shrink during illness, teething, travel, nursery changes or a nap transition.

Do not rebuild the schedule from two unusual mornings. Separate ordinary home days from disruption days.

### 6. The first nap is carrying sleep debt

An early, long first nap can become recovery sleep after repeated early mornings. That may be helpful in the short term. But if the cycle becomes entrenched—very early wake, very early long nap, then another early wake the next day—it can be worth reviewing the whole 24-hour pattern rather than stretching only the first window.

## What different short windows may mean

These ranges are observations, not age prescriptions.

| Pattern | What to consider | Gentle next step |
|---|---|---|
| asleep again within 10–30 minutes, barely alert | partial waking or morning resettle | keep things dark and quiet; let the sleep continue safely |
| asleep again within 30–60 minutes after little care | the end of night sleep may have been logged too early | compare the second wake with the baby’s usual true morning state |
| asleep within 30–60 minutes after a full feed, change and alert play | a real but short first window, or recovery after a poor night | log Nap 1 and compare with the preceding night |
| first window suddenly much shorter for one or two days | illness, teething, developmental change or sleep debt | follow cues and hold the schedule loosely |
| first window is short every day but baby is content | this may be their current rhythm | leave it alone unless the nap or rest of the day is difficult |
| first window shortens while feeding, nappies or alertness worsen | possible illness or feeding problem | seek health advice rather than changing sleep timing |

Avoid treating 45, 60 or 90 minutes as a universal cut-off. Age, prematurity, night quality and the baby’s state all matter.

## Should I try to stretch the first wake window?

Only when all of these are true:

- you are confident the baby was fully awake for the day
- the pattern has repeated on at least three comparable mornings
- baby is otherwise well
- the early nap is difficult, very short or pulling the whole day earlier
- a slightly longer calm awake period seems tolerable

Then try **5–10 minutes later** for three mornings. Use low-key activity: open the curtains, move to another room, cuddle at a window, sing or spend a few minutes on the floor. Stop when tired cues become clear.

Do not use bright screens, vigorous stimulation or distress to “make” a baby last. If settling becomes harder, the nap shortens or baby wakes crying, return to the earlier timing.

For babies over roughly four months, a modest gradual shift may be useful. For newborns and babies with feeding or growth concerns, get individual advice rather than extending awake time.

## When not to change anything

Leave the first window alone when:

- baby falls asleep easily and wakes restored
- the nap fits the rest of the day
- bedtime and nights are workable
- the pattern has existed only briefly
- baby is recovering from illness, vaccination, travel or a bad night
- the chart is the only reason you think it is wrong

A shorter first window is not a developmental failure. The goal is a baby who copes comfortably, not equal-sized gaps on a spreadsheet.

## A four-morning pattern check

Track only the information that answers the question.

### Each morning, record

- the final bedtime and any substantial night wakes
- the first stir you considered a morning wake
- state: drowsy, quiet-alert or fully active
- feed or nappy care between the two sleeps
- when sleep restarted and ended
- whether the sleep needed a full nap routine
- wake mood after the second sleep
- the rest of the day’s nap count and bedtime
- context such as illness, teething, travel or nursery

### Then compare like with like

Ask:

1. Did the shortest first windows follow the most fragmented nights?
2. Was baby ever clearly alert before the early sleep?
3. Did the second wake look more like the real start of the day?
4. Did moving Nap 1 later improve settling and nap quality—or make both worse?
5. Is the pattern stable enough to act on, or is every morning different?

One morning can be a story. Four ordinary mornings can begin to show a pattern.

## Common mistakes that make the pattern harder to read

### Starting the day at every noise

Babies can move, fuss briefly or open their eyes in light sleep. Stay present and responsive, but avoid switching on the day before they are clearly awake.

### Treating every feed as proof the day started

A baby can feed and return to sleep while the household remains in night mode. The feed matters; it does not automatically turn the next sleep into a daytime nap.

### Stretching after one short morning

One early nap after a rough night is often recovery. Moving it later can create an overtired, harder-to-settle baby without teaching you anything about their usual rhythm.

### Comparing Nap 1 with the final wake window

Awake stretches do not have to be equal. The late-day window often carries different sleep pressure, light exposure and family activity. Compare first windows with other first windows.

### Letting the app override the baby

A prediction is a preparation window, not permission. If your baby is clearly tired, begin the wind-down. If they are bright and content, allow the morning to breathe.

## Safe sleep for the return sleep

Whether you call it night sleep, a morning resettle or Nap 1, safer-sleep guidance is the same.

- place baby on their back at the beginning of every sleep
- use a clear, flat, firm separate sleep space
- keep pillows, duvets, bumpers, pods, nests, positioners and soft toys out
- keep baby in the same room as you for every sleep for the first six months
- never fall asleep with a baby on a sofa or armchair

If baby dozes in your arms after a morning feed and you feel sleepy, move them to their safer sleep space before you drift off.

## When unusual morning sleepiness needs medical advice

A familiar, comfortable short first wake window is different from a baby who is unexpectedly difficult to wake or not behaving normally.

Seek prompt medical advice if your baby:

- is hard to wake, floppy, confused or unusually unresponsive
- is not feeding normally and you are worried
- has nappies that are drier than usual
- has rapid or difficult breathing, grunting or the skin pulling in under the ribs
- has blue, grey, very pale or blotchy skin, lips or tongue
- has repeated vomiting or green vomit
- cries constantly and cannot be consoled, or the cry sounds unlike normal

Call 999 if your baby will not wake, is struggling to breathe, has a seizure or you think there is an emergency. For a baby under three months, a temperature of **38°C or above** needs urgent medical assessment.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the first wake window supposed to be the shortest?

It often is for some babies, but it is not a rule. The first window may be short after a fragmented night, during a transition or because the first logged wake was only a morning stir. Follow your baby’s state and repeated pattern.

### Does a feed reset the wake window?

No. Wake time is measured from the point the baby is genuinely awake, not from the end of a feed. A sleepy feed can happen inside a brief night waking; an alert feed can be part of the daytime window.

### My baby wakes at 6am and sleeps again at 7am. Is 7am Nap 1?

It depends. If baby was alert, fed, changed and interacted, Nap 1 may be reasonable. If they stayed drowsy in low light and drifted back easily, the 7am sleep may be the end of night. Watch how they wake afterwards and what repeats.

### Should I wake my baby from the early first nap?

Usually not solely to protect a chart. Consider the baby’s age, total day sleep, bedtime and any clinical feeding plan. If a long first nap repeatedly pulls the whole day early or affects later sleep, review the pattern before capping it.

### Why did the first wake window suddenly shrink?

Common context includes a poor night, illness, teething, travel, developmental change or an earlier morning start. Follow tired cues for a few days. Seek advice if the change comes with unusual sleepiness, poor feeding, fewer wet nappies or illness signs.

### When does the first wake window get longer?

Awake tolerance generally grows over time, but not on a fixed date. Look for a calm, repeated shift across weeks. OBubba’s Flutter engine waits for at least ten sampled days across two comparison periods and a meaningful increase before calling it maturation.

### What if the early nap is always only 20–30 minutes?

If baby was hard to settle and wakes upset, the preceding window may have been too long despite looking short—or discomfort may be involved. If baby took a long time to settle and wakes bright, they may not have had enough sleep pressure. Change timing by only 5–10 minutes and compare several mornings.

## The bottom line

A short first wake window does not automatically need stretching. Decide whether the day truly started, respond to feeding and care needs, and compare the same morning sequence across several ordinary days.

If baby barely woke and returned to sleep, you may be looking at the final chapter of the night. If they were clearly alert, it may simply be a short Nap 1 lead-in—and that can be completely workable.
