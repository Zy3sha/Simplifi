You fed the baby, remembered the vitamin drops, noticed the next packet of nappies was nearly finished, booked the health visitor appointment, washed the bottle parts and knew which kind of rocking worked at 3am. By breakfast, it can somehow feel as though you have “done nothing”.

That is the strange arithmetic of early parenthood: essential care happens in dozens of small, repeating moments, while much of the thinking behind it stays invisible.

**The goal is not to prove who worked hardest. It is to make enough of the workload visible that one person does not have to notice, remember, decide, perform and explain everything alone.**

## What is the new-parent mental load?

Baby care has at least three overlapping layers:

| Layer | What it can include | Why it is easy to miss |
|---|---|---|
| **Hands-on care** | feeds, nappies, settling, baths, contact naps, medicine and washing equipment | each task looks small when viewed alone |
| **Cognitive work** | noticing supplies, anticipating the next feed, remembering advice, planning appointments and watching patterns | it happens inside one person’s head |
| **Emotional work** | staying calm, reassuring a worried partner, making decisions while tired and absorbing family opinions | it rarely appears on a to-do list |

The phrase *mental load* usually points most directly to that middle layer: being the household’s memory, alert system and decision-maker. But the physical and emotional layers feed it. A parent who has soothed four wakes is not only tired from being awake; they may also be calculating when the baby last fed, whether the nappy was wet and how the morning will work.

This is why “just tell me what to do” does not always feel like shared care. Giving instructions is another task.

## First, stop using tiredness as a fairness calculator

When both adults are exhausted, a conversation can turn into competing evidence:

- “I did the last wake.”
- “But I was up expressing.”
- “I have work in the morning.”
- “I have the baby all day.”

Those statements may all be true. The problem is that exhaustion makes each person see the work they performed and the rest they missed. It is much harder to see everything the other person carried.

The NHS notes that having a baby often strains relationships partly because parents are tired, have less time and may resent an apparent lack of support. It also makes a blunt but useful point: even a close partner cannot read your mind. A shared system should reduce the amount that needs to be silently remembered, not become evidence for a nightly prosecution.

Try this opening instead:

> “I do not need us to decide who has it worse. I need us to see what must happen tonight and make sure neither of us is carrying all of it.”

## A ten-minute care-load reset

Do this during daylight if possible—not at 2am, and not in the middle of a crying spell.

### 1. Name the recurring work

Write down the care that repeats in your household. Keep it specific:

- breastfeeding, bottles or pumping
- washing and preparing feeding equipment
- settling after feeds
- nappies and outfit changes
- naps and contact naps
- bedtime and overnight wakes
- medicines, vitamins or symptom notes
- ordering supplies
- appointments and questions for professionals
- laundry, meals and care of older children
- updating grandparents, nursery or another carer

If one parent is breastfeeding or recovering from birth, “equal” cannot mean identical tasks. The useful question is what the other adult can fully own around that non-transferable work: bringing food and water, changing and settling the baby, washing parts, protecting a rest block or managing the morning handover.

### 2. Separate ownership from assistance

“Helping with bottles” may still leave one person checking whether bottles are clean, deciding when to prepare one and asking for help each time.

Full ownership sounds more like:

- “I own bottle washing and sterilising until lunchtime.”
- “I handle the first resettle after each feed tonight.”
- “I check the shared timeline before asking when the baby last slept.”
- “I monitor nappy supplies and reorder them.”

Ownership includes noticing and completing. It should also include knowing when professional advice or the baby’s cues override the household plan.

### 3. Protect one real rest opportunity

“Rest when you can” is too vague. Decide who is off duty, when the block begins, what would justify waking them and who owns the baby during it.

A rest block might be:

- one adult covering the baby after an early feed
- a partner doing the nappy, winding and resettling around a breastfeed
- a trusted relative taking a supervised daytime care window
- one parent sleeping first while the other handles the evening

Do not promise a perfect number of hours that a newborn cannot guarantee. Do create a handover clear enough that the resting parent is not still answering every question.

### 4. Decide the minimum worth logging

A shared record is helpful when it prevents duplicate questions and unsafe guessing. It is not helpful when a recovering parent becomes the unpaid data administrator.

Prioritise events another carer genuinely needs:

- when and how the baby fed
- the measured amount actually taken from a bottle—not an invented breastfeeding volume
- wet and dirty nappies when intake or illness is a concern
- sleep start and end times if shifts depend on them
- medicine name, amount and exact time, checked against the label or professional advice
- temperature, symptoms or a reaction that may need escalation

Skip decorative precision. “About 2:10am” is often enough for a sleep log. Medicine and safety information need exact confirmation.

### 5. Review the system, not the person

After two or three days, ask:

- Which question did we keep asking?
- Which task still depended on one person noticing?
- Did either person get a protected break?
- What can be removed, automated or owned completely?

That makes the household process the thing being improved. It avoids turning one exhausted night into a verdict on either parent.

## What a baby-care total can—and cannot—tell you

A month of feeds, naps and nappies can be surprisingly moving when you see it together. It can confirm that the repetitive work was real. It can also reveal that a “quiet” month contained hundreds of moments of care.

But a total cannot tell you:

- who noticed the task first
- how difficult a particular feed or wake felt
- who was recovering, unwell or working elsewhere
- how long someone spent worrying, planning or seeking help
- whether labour was fairly shared
- whether you are a good parent

Counts should be a mirror, not a leaderboard. If they start prompting comparison, guilt or compulsive logging, simplify the record and return to the practical question: **what would make care safer and more sustainable today?**

## If the load is becoming unsafe

Mental load is not only a relationship inconvenience. Severe tiredness can affect concentration, decisions and physical safety.

If you might fall asleep while holding or feeding your baby, do not sit on a sofa or armchair with them. NHS safer-sleep guidance says these are particularly dangerous places to fall asleep with a baby. Put the baby in a clear, separate, firm and flat sleep space on their back, or move to a safer plan before you become overwhelmed by sleepiness. Discuss your family’s individual sleep situation with your midwife or health visitor.

Ask for same-day practical help when:

- nobody is alert enough to drive or care safely
- you are repeatedly nodding off in unsafe places
- one adult has had no meaningful rest and there is no workable handover
- anger, panic or frightening thoughts are escalating
- feeding or recovery problems are preventing either parent from functioning

If you are reaching breaking point during crying, place the baby safely in their cot, step away briefly and call another adult. Never shake a baby.

## Mental health needs care, not a better spreadsheet

The NHS says difficult feelings after a baby can affect mothers, fathers, partners and carers. Speak to your GP, midwife or health visitor if low mood, anxiety, irritability, hopelessness, guilt, difficulty bonding or an inability to sleep even when given the chance is persisting, worsening or making it hard to cope.

Call **999** or go to **A&E** if you think there is an immediate risk that you may harm yourself or your baby. For an urgent mental health crisis that is not an immediate emergency, use **NHS 111 online** or call **111** and select the mental health option where available in your area.

No tracker can diagnose postnatal depression, guarantee safe care or decide whether your workload is fair. A log can make a pattern easier to describe. It should never become a reason to wait for help.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should couples split baby care 50/50?

Not necessarily task by task. Birth recovery, breastfeeding, employment, disability, health and family structure affect what each person can do. Aim for a safe and sustainable division in which both adults get care, information and genuine off-duty time. Revisit it as the baby changes.

### How do I ask my partner to take more mental load?

Ask them to own a complete area rather than “help more”. Name the result, the boundary and when it will be reviewed: “Please own bottle washing and stock until Sunday; that includes noticing what is running low.” Use a shared timeline so they can answer routine questions without routing everything through you.

### Is tracking every feed and nappy necessary?

No. Detailed newborn logs can help with feeding assessment, handovers or medical questions, but healthy older babies may need much less. Log what changes a decision or reduces repeated questions. Follow individual advice from your midwife, health visitor, GP or neonatal team.

### Can OBubba show which parent did each task?

The monthly **A Month of Love** card summarises the baby’s care record; it is not a contributor score or time-and-motion audit. Use shared logging for continuity and handovers, not to rank carers.

### What if I am parenting without a partner?

Shared care can involve a relative, friend, doula, nursery or other trusted carer. Ask for concrete ownership—food delivery, laundry, an accompanied rest window, school pickup or one regular check-in. Your health visitor can also connect you with local services and Family Hubs.
