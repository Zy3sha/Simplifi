You stirred a little peanut butter smoothly into porridge. Your baby touched the spoon to their tongue, pulled a magnificent face and pushed everything back out. Did peanut just move from “not tried” to “introduced”?

**The short answer:** a lick or uncertain taste tells you the food was *offered*, but it does not prove that a meaningful amount was swallowed or tolerated. If you clearly saw your baby swallow a small amount and no symptoms followed, record exactly that. Do not turn one tick, one lick or one symptom-free meal into a guarantee that an allergy is impossible.

The NHS does not give every baby one universal spoonful that “counts”. It advises introducing foods that can trigger allergy from around six months, **one at a time and in very small amounts**, then keeping tolerated foods in the usual diet. That makes honest observation more useful than chasing a magic dose.

> **A calm rule:** record what happened—not what you hoped the attempt meant.

## Offered, tasted, eaten or tolerated?

These words sound interchangeable at the highchair. They are not.

| What actually happened | The most honest record | What it does **not** establish |
|---|---|---|
| food touched the hand, cheek or lips only | **offered / explored** | ingestion or tolerance |
| baby licked it, then spat it out; you are unsure anything was swallowed | **tasted; swallowed amount uncertain** | a completed introduction |
| baby clearly swallowed a small amount and remained well | **ate a small amount; no reaction observed** | lifelong tolerance or a medical allergy test |
| baby ate it and developed possible symptoms | **suspected reaction**, with food, amount, time and symptoms | that it is safe to try again at home |
| baby already has a diagnosed allergy or clinician-led plan | follow that individual plan | that generic internet advice overrides their clinician |

“No immediate reaction” is more precise than “safe”. It leaves room for uncertainty, later reactions and the fact that an app cannot diagnose food allergy.

## What if my baby licked it and spat it out?

That is normal first-food behaviour. Babies are learning how food feels, moves and leaves the mouth. They may smear, lick, gag, push food forwards with the tongue or drop it without taking a real mouthful.

Do not force another spoon in to make the attempt count. Instead:

1. end the attempt if your baby has had enough
2. write “licked; unsure swallowed”
3. keep watching for symptoms because some contact may still have occurred
4. if your baby remains well and is otherwise suitable for home introduction, offer the food again on another calm day

Smearing food on the skin is not a useful home allergy test and may irritate sensitive skin. Introduce food by mouth in an age-appropriate form unless a clinician has given your baby a different plan.

## A low-drama first-allergen plan

### 1. Start when your baby is ready

For most babies, allergenic foods can be introduced from around six months, alongside other solids, once the baby can stay sitting with a steady head, coordinate food to the mouth and swallow rather than automatically push everything out.

Your baby should be awake, well and not exhausted. Milk remains the main drink throughout the first year; an allergen introduction is food practice, not a test your baby has to pass.

If your baby has severe eczema, an existing food allergy, or you have been told they are at higher risk, ask your GP, health visitor or allergy team how and when to introduce egg or peanut. Use their plan over this general guide.

### 2. Choose one new allergen

Introduce one new allergenic food at a time so a reaction is easier to connect to the likely food. You do not need an otherwise empty menu: serve it alongside foods your baby already eats comfortably.

Choose a time when an adult can stay present and access help. Earlier in the day is often more practical than just before bed, nursery or a long journey.

### 3. Make the texture safe

The allergy question never replaces choking prevention.

- cook egg until the white and yolk are solid, unless using a product made under British Lion Code guidance
- thin smooth peanut or nut butter into porridge, yoghurt or puree; do not offer a sticky spoonful
- never give whole nuts to a baby
- remove fish bones and prepare every food in a texture your baby can manage
- sit your baby upright and supervise throughout

Our [foods babies should avoid guide](/blog/foods-babies-should-avoid-uk-weaning-safety-list.html) separates allergy, choking, salt, sugar and infection risks so one safety concern does not hide another.

### 4. Begin small and follow the baby

Offer a very small amount. Let your baby accept, pause or refuse. If they swallow it comfortably and stay well, you can gradually offer more as part of the meal rather than forcing a large portion at once.

There is no prize for finishing the bowl. A useful first entry might be:

> **Peanut:** smooth peanut butter thinned into familiar porridge; swallowed two small spoonfuls at 9:15am; no immediate reaction observed.

If the only truthful entry is “touched lips and spat out”, use that instead.

### 5. Watch the baby, not only the stopwatch

Most food-allergy reactions happen within minutes and can occur up to two hours after eating. Some reactions—particularly some cow's-milk reactions—can be delayed for longer. Note the time the food was eaten and what changed.

A symptom-free observation period is helpful information, not proof that every future serving will be safe. If the food was clearly eaten and tolerated, NHS guidance is to keep it in the baby's usual diet rather than treating one introduction as a finished job.

## What symptoms should I look for?

Possible food-allergy symptoms can include:

- swollen lips or face
- red, itchy or watery eyes
- an itchy, raised rash or hives
- coughing, wheezing or noisy breathing
- vomiting, tummy pain, diarrhoea or constipation
- worsening eczema

Stop the food and seek medical advice if you suspect a reaction. Do not re-offer it to “check” what happened.

Call **999** if your baby is struggling to breathe, their tongue or throat is swelling, they become suddenly floppy, faint or unusually unresponsive, or you think they may be having anaphylaxis. Follow any prescribed allergy action plan and use prescribed adrenaline exactly as trained.

A red mark only where food touched the skin can be contact irritation rather than allergy, especially around the mouth—but a photo and a precise record are still useful. Ask a health professional rather than diagnosing it from appearance alone.

## Why one tolerated taste is only the beginning

Once a food has been eaten without a reaction, keep serving it as part of the normal diet in an age-appropriate form. OBubba's current Flutter Allergen Journey uses a practical reminder of about twice a week for foods already introduced, but that is an organisational prompt—not an individual prescription.

How often, how much and in what form may need adapting for your baby's age, appetite, eczema, previous symptoms and clinical advice. If the food disappears from the menu for weeks because life got busy, simply note the gap and ask for advice if your baby has a complex allergy history.

## A handover another carer can understand

“Tried peanut” is too vague for a partner, nursery or grandparent. Use four details:

1. **food and form** — smooth peanut butter thinned into porridge
2. **what was eaten** — licked only, one spoon swallowed, or portion eaten
3. **when** — the meal time
4. **what followed** — no immediate reaction observed, or the exact symptom and time

That one line prevents a tick being interpreted as “definitely safe” and stops the same uncertain introduction being repeated without context.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does food touching my baby's lips count as exposure?

It is contact, but it does not establish that food was eaten or tolerated. Record the contact honestly and watch for symptoms. Do not rub food on the skin as a home allergy test.

### What if I do not know whether my baby swallowed any?

Write “tasted; amount swallowed uncertain”. Do not force more. If your baby remains well and is suitable for home introduction, offer the food again at a practical time. Ask a clinician first if your baby is higher risk or has had previous symptoms.

### How much allergen must my baby eat for it to count?

There is no single universal amount in NHS guidance that proves tolerance for every baby and food. Begin with a very small, safely prepared amount, follow your baby, and build gradually if there is no reaction. A clinician-led introduction may specify exact amounts; follow that plan.

### If my baby ate it once without symptoms, are they not allergic?

One symptom-free serving is reassuring information, but it is not a diagnostic test or lifetime guarantee. Keep the tolerated food in the usual diet and seek advice if symptoms occur on a later exposure.

### Should I introduce egg and peanut on the same day?

Introduce new allergenic foods one at a time. Serving a new allergen with already tolerated foods is fine; introducing two new allergens together makes any reaction harder to interpret.

### Can OBubba tell whether my baby tolerated an allergen?

No. OBubba can organise food and reaction records, recognise allergen groups and help you remember what may be next. It cannot observe swallowing, assess symptoms, diagnose allergy or declare a food safe.

## The bottom line

A lick counts as exploration. A clearly swallowed small amount is an eaten exposure. Neither is a certificate of lifelong tolerance.

Prepare the food safely, offer one new allergen at a time, let your baby lead, and record what actually entered the mouth and what happened afterwards. If there is a possible reaction, stop and get advice rather than running a second experiment at home.

The best allergen tracker is not the one with the fastest row of ticks. It is the one another tired parent—or a clinician—can read and trust.
