## The quick answer: read what happens to the record, not just the word “private”

A pregnancy or baby app may hold due dates, feeds, sleep, nappies, medicine notes, growth records and family details. Before adding that history, find clear answers to eight questions:

1. What works without an account?
2. What stays on the device, and what goes to cloud storage?
3. Who can see a shared baby record, and how is access removed?
4. Does any AI feature receive a question or baby-log context?
5. What analytics or advertising technology is used?
6. Is location required, optional or stored?
7. Can the family export its history in a usable format?
8. Can the account and cloud data be deleted without contacting support?

The best answer is not always “nothing ever leaves the phone”. Sync, backup and partner sharing need data to move somewhere. The useful test is whether the app explains each transfer plainly, limits it to the job requested and gives the family a meaningful choice.

## 1. Can you begin without creating an account?

An account may be needed for backup, a second phone or a partner invitation. It should not be treated as invisible plumbing. Check whether the core record can begin locally, what changes after sign-in and which details the account requires.

Ask:

- Can I inspect the app before giving an email address?
- Does creating an account automatically enable cloud backup or sharing?
- Is the account for the adult, rather than a public profile for the baby?

A baby tracker should not make a child publicly discoverable just because a parent wants a private family record.

## 2. Where is each kind of information stored?

“Stored securely” is too vague on its own. Look for a privacy policy that separates local device storage, optional cloud backup and any third-party processing.

For cloud storage, the policy should identify the provider or at least the type of service, explain why the transfer happens and say how deletion works. Encryption in transit is useful, but it is not the whole privacy story: access control, retention and account recovery also matter.

The UK Information Commissioner’s Office emphasises data minimisation, transparency and high-privacy defaults in its [Children’s code](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/). A parenting app used by adults is not automatically the same as a child-facing service, but those principles are still sensible questions for any product holding information about a baby.

## 3. How does family sharing actually work?

“Share with your village” can describe very different systems. Before inviting somebody, check:

- whether access is invitation-only;
- whether the invited person sees the whole record or a limited handover;
- whether they can add or edit entries;
- whether a link can be forwarded;
- who can revoke access; and
- whether a former partner or carer remains connected after the handover ends.

Two parents updating one record is different from giving a grandparent a temporary browser view. The app should explain that difference before the family shares anything.

For a practical comparison, see [one live record for two parents](/partner-baby-tracker-app.html) and [a no-login handover for a trusted carer](/baby-tracker-for-grandparents.html).

## 4. What does an AI feature receive?

The label “AI” does not tell you where processing happens. Built-in rules may run locally. A conversational answer may send the typed question and selected context to an external model provider.

Before enabling an AI feature, look for four specifics:

- the provider’s name;
- the exact categories sent, such as recent sleep or feed times;
- whether the feature is off until consent; and
- where to turn it off later.

Do not assume that removing a name makes every care detail anonymous. A dated log can still be personal information. An app should send only the context needed for the answer and should not hide the transfer behind a generic “personalisation” switch.

## 5. Are analytics and advertising kept separate from baby data?

Analytics can help a team learn whether a feature opens or fails. Advertising systems can create a much wider trail. Look for direct answers to these questions:

- Does the app contain third-party advertising?
- Is baby or care data included in analytics events?
- Is behaviour used for targeted advertising or cross-app tracking?
- Can non-essential measurement be declined?

A policy that simply lists an analytics provider is incomplete if it never explains what event data is sent. Equally, the presence of privacy-friendly aggregate measurement does not prove an install, a health outcome or that no technical request data is processed.

## 6. Is location optional and limited to a visible job?

Most baby logging does not need precise location. If an app requests it, the reason should be visible at the moment of the request, and the app should still be useful when permission is denied unless location is essential to that feature.

Check whether coordinates are stored, used only in real time, shared with a mapping provider or used for marketing. The ICO’s public guidance recommends checking settings whenever a child uses a new app or device and highlights high-privacy defaults and non-essential location tracking being switched off in child-accessible services: [Children’s code and children’s online privacy](https://ico.org.uk/for-the-public/the-children-s-code-and-children-s-online-privacy/).

## 7. Can you take the history with you?

A family record becomes more valuable over time. That makes portability important.

Look for an export that produces a readable file such as CSV, not just a screenshot. Check whether the export includes the categories you rely on and whether you can create it without asking the company to perform a manual favour. If you switch trackers, keep the original export until you have checked dates, units and skipped rows in the new app.

OBubba publishes a [preview-first guide to importing baby-tracker history](/import-baby-tracker-data.html); the page accepts no upload and explains why a best-effort migration still needs checking.

## 8. Can you delete the account and cloud copy?

Uninstalling an app may remove local files while leaving a cloud account intact. A clear policy should distinguish:

- deleting an entry;
- deleting one baby profile;
- signing out;
- removing the app from the phone; and
- deleting the account and associated cloud data.

Check whether deletion is available inside the app, whether there is a delay or legal retention exception and what happens to data previously shared with another family member. Do this before you need it, not after a phone is lost or a relationship changes.

## A 60-second comparison card

When comparing two pregnancy or baby apps, write one line for each item:

| Question | App A | App B |
|---|---|---|
| Works before account creation |  |  |
| Local versus cloud storage explained |  |  |
| Sharing is invitation-based and revocable |  |  |

Leave a box blank when the answer is hard to find. Missing information is itself useful information when choosing where to place a long family history.

## Choose clarity over slogans

No app can truthfully promise that a connected service is impossible to breach. A better sign is specific, current information: what is collected, where it goes, why it goes there, who can access it and how the family can export or delete it.

Read the policy before building a long record. Then use only the features that give your family something back.
