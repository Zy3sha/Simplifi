You place your baby on their back, say goodnight and reach the door. Behind you: rustle, shuffle, pull—and a very proud face appears above the cot rail.

You lie them down. They stand again. The third time, the pride turns to tears because they have learnt **up** but not yet worked out **down**.

What should you do when your baby keeps standing in the cot at bedtime?

**Make the cot safe for a standing baby first. Then practise lowering from standing during calm daytime play and use one boring, predictable bedtime response.** If baby is happily practising in a safe cot, you do not have to repeatedly place them down. If they are upset or genuinely stuck, calmly help them bend, lower and settle—without turning it into a game.

> **Standing is a new skill, not bedtime defiance. Safety first; practice by day; calm help at night.**

Research does suggest that a new motor milestone can coincide with sleep disruption, but it is not a universal “regression”. One small longitudinal study followed 20 babies and found pull-to-stand was linked with increased night waking only among the earlier achievers. Your baby's standing may be relevant; it is not automatically the whole explanation.

## Do this before the next sleep

When pulling to stand arrives, the sleep space needs a fresh safety check.

1. **Read the cot instructions today.** Move the mattress base to the lowest appropriate position before baby can climb or fall over the side. Health Canada advises the lowest level once a baby can sit upright or push onto hands and knees; the manufacturer's limits for your product still apply.
2. **Remove everything baby could use as a step.** No pillows, bumpers, toys, folded blankets, positioners or nests.
3. **Check the reach zone.** Move blind cords, monitor leads, lamps, wall art, shelves, toiletries and furniture beyond reach—even if they were unreachable last week.
4. **Check the cot itself.** Tighten fixings according to the instructions and stop using it if parts, slats or the mattress support are damaged or missing.
5. **Keep the mattress firm, flat and close-fitting.** Use a fitted sheet only, or safe lightweight bedding according to current guidance.
6. **Place baby on their back to begin every sleep.** Do not wedge, strap or hold them in position.

The NHS says the safest place for a baby to sleep for the first six months is a cot in the same room as a parent, on their back, with a firm, flat mattress and a clear sleep space. The Lullaby Trust's “clear cot” advice excludes pillows, duvets, bumpers, soft toys, weighted bedding, pods, nests and positioners.

## Are they practising—or asking for help?

The same standing posture can mean different things.

| What you see | More like calm practice | More like stuck or distressed |
|---|---|---|
| face and voice | curious, babbling, smiling or quiet | escalating cry, frightened expression or calling |
| movement | shifts weight, bounces, reaches or explores the rail | grips rigidly, legs look locked, cannot bend or release |
| attempts to lower | experiments with one hand, knee or bottom | repeatedly tries and panics, or makes no safe attempt |
| what happens if you pause nearby | continues calmly | distress grows or baby is losing balance |
| response | allow safe practice; keep the room boring | go in, reassure and help baby lower safely |

Do not make a distressed baby prove they can get down. Helping a genuinely stuck baby does not “reward” a milestone. Equally, repeatedly rushing in to lie down a cheerful standing baby can create a fascinating parent-return game.

## Why “up” can arrive before “down”

Pulling to stand and lowering are related but separate motor problems.

To stand, a baby learns to:

- grip a stable surface
- move through kneeling or half-kneeling
- shift weight over one foot
- extend the hips and knees
- use the arms and legs together

To get down in control, they must reverse or adapt that sequence:

- bend at the hips and knees
- keep balance while the body descends
- release one hand at the right moment
- rotate toward sitting or kneeling
- judge where the floor is without looking directly at it

Somerset NHS explains that babies may begin pulling to stand across a wide range and, at first, may not sit back down in control—they may “plop” onto their bottom. That mismatch is why a baby can stand confidently and still feel stranded.

It is not stubbornness. It is an unfinished movement sequence.

## The daytime “bend, lower, sit” practice

Practise when baby is rested, supervised and barefoot or in secure non-slip clothing on a firm floor. A stable low sofa is usually easier than the cot because you can support from beside and behind.

### Step 1: start low

Let baby play in kneeling at a stable surface. Place one interesting toy low enough that they must bend slightly, not stretch upward.

### Step 2: move the prize down

While baby stands, move a toy from the surface toward the floor beside one foot. The aim is a small knee bend and downward look, not a dramatic squat.

### Step 3: guide at the hips

Place your hands around the hips, not on the arms. Gently guide the hips backward and down while saying one consistent cue such as “bend, down, sit”. Let baby do as much of the movement as possible.

### Step 4: help one knee soften

If both legs stay straight, encourage weight onto one side so the other knee can bend. Guide toward kneeling or side-sitting rather than pulling baby abruptly away from the furniture.

### Step 5: repeat both sides

Practise lowering to the left and right. Two or three successful repetitions are enough; stop before tiredness turns practice into frustration.

Leeds Community Healthcare physiotherapy guidance recommends practising the full sequence—kneeling, pulling to stand and lowering from standing—and guiding the child's weight and hips rather than doing the movement for them.

Do not use a baby walker to teach the skill. NHS physiotherapy sources note that walkers and bouncers do not teach moving into and out of standing and may delay independent standing skills.

## A calm response at bedtime

Choose a response you can repeat at 7pm and 2am.

### If baby stands happily

- keep lights low and your voice quiet
- give them a little safe space to experiment
- do not restart songs, stories or play
- avoid eye-catching reactions from the doorway
- let them lower themselves if they can

Your job is not to make baby remain horizontal. It is to provide a safe sleep opportunity.

### If baby is upset or stuck

1. go in calmly
2. use the same short phrase: “You're safe; bend and sit”
3. guide at the hips so the knees bend
4. help them move to sitting, then place them on their back
5. offer the usual brief reassurance
6. leave or step back in the same way you normally do

If they stand again and cry, repeat with as little variation as possible. Boring consistency makes the response predictable without withholding help.

### If every lay-down creates a bigger game

Stop trying to win the up-down contest. Once the cot is safe, help only when baby is distressed or unable to lower. A calm standing baby may eventually sit, slump or lie down when the novelty fades.

Never push a baby down, prise their fingers from the rail, pin their body to the mattress or add a device intended to keep them lying down.

## Should you keep laying baby back down?

Use the least help that keeps baby safe and emotionally supported.

| Situation | Sensible response | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| calm, stable standing | pause and observe; keep bedtime dull | repeated dramatic returns |
| upset and cannot lower | guide hips down, reassure, place on back | leaving a panicked baby stuck |
| lowers, then stands for fun | give space unless distress returns | turning it into a chase or game |
| repeatedly falls hard | practise lowering by day and check the cot setup | adding padded bumpers or pillows |
| tries to climb or gets a leg over | follow cot limits and consider the next safe sleep setup promptly | crib tents, restraints or makeshift higher sides |
| unusual stiffness, weakness or asymmetry | seek professional assessment | assuming every movement difference is “just a phase” |

The answer may change across the week. A baby who needs hands-on help on Monday may lower independently by Friday.

## Will more daytime standing make bedtime worse?

Ordinary, supervised floor play usually helps because baby gets repeated chances to complete the whole sequence. The useful practice is not endless upright time; it is **moving into and out of positions**.

Prioritise:

- free movement on a firm floor
- kneeling at stable furniture
- pulling up from the baby's own effort
- reaching low while standing
- lowering into kneeling or side-sitting
- crawling, shuffling or other self-chosen movement

Limit time in equipment that holds the baby upright without requiring balance or transitions. The goal is not to exhaust the skill out of them before bed; it is to make “down” as familiar as “up”.

Finish energetic practice before the wind-down begins. The last 20–30 minutes can return to dim light, quiet connection and the usual routine.

## Is this really a sleep regression?

Maybe—but that phrase can hide more than it explains.

The strongest direct study on pull-to-stand and sleep was small. Researchers followed 20 healthy babies from seven to roughly twelve months using home motor observations and actigraphy. Pull-to-stand coincided with increased night waking among babies who achieved the skill early, but not those who achieved it later.

A separate intensive time-series study followed only three families but collected hundreds of days of daily sleep and motor data. It found the timing and direction of sleep changes varied by baby and by milestone.

That means:

- new motor learning **can** coincide with disrupted sleep
- the link is not inevitable
- standing in the cot may be the visible symptom while timing, hunger, teething or separation also contributes
- a recent milestone is context, not a diagnosis

If naps, the final wake window, bedtime, feeding or illness also changed, review those alongside the standing.

## A seven-night pattern check

Track enough to separate skill practice from a broader sleep problem.

| Record | Why it helps |
|---|---|
| date baby first pulled to stand | anchors the new-skill window |
| bedtime and actual sleep onset | shows whether standing is delaying sleep |
| number of stand-ups | reveals whether novelty is fading |
| calm practice or distressed/stuck | separates exploration from need for help |
| how baby lowered | self, guided hips, lifted or fell to bottom |
| final awake stretch and naps | tests sleep pressure alongside the milestone |
| night wakes with standing | shows whether the behaviour affects only bedtime or the whole night |
| teething, illness and separation context | prevents every difficult night being blamed on movement |

Review after a week. Useful progress might be:

- fewer distressed stand-ups
- one independent lower
- shorter delay before sleep
- standing remains but baby is calm
- bedtime improves while nights remain unchanged

Do not require zero standing. Mastery often looks like less distress before it looks like less practice.

## When to get advice

Wide variation is normal, and some babies crawl, shuffle or pull to stand in a different order. Speak to your health visitor, GP or paediatric physiotherapy team if:

- baby seems unusually stiff or floppy
- one side consistently moves differently from the other
- baby cannot bear weight in a way they previously could
- movement appears painful
- a previously gained skill is lost
- you have broader concerns about development, feeding, alertness or growth
- the cot behaviour includes repeated injury or a fall

Seek urgent help for a serious fall, loss of consciousness, seizure, breathing difficulty, unusual drowsiness, repeated vomiting after a head injury or another emergency.

## Cot safety once standing begins

Re-check the environment every week because reach changes quickly.

- keep the mattress base at the lowest appropriate level according to the instructions
- keep the cot away from windows, blind cords, monitor cables, shelves, lamps and furniture
- remove mobiles and toy bars once baby can push up or reach them
- keep the cot empty of objects that can become steps
- never add bumpers to soften falls
- do not use cot tents, ties, restraints or makeshift rail extensions
- move to the next appropriate sleep product when baby reaches the cot's height, weight, climbing or other usage limit

Safety takes priority over preserving a familiar-looking nursery.

## Frequently asked questions

### My baby stands and cries every time I lay them down. Should I leave them?

If they are distressed or cannot lower, help calmly. Guide the hips down, use one brief phrase and return to the usual bedtime response. If they stand happily, a short pause in a safe cot may prevent an up-down game.

### How long does the standing-in-cot phase last?

There is no fixed timetable. It may ease over days as lowering becomes automatic, or recur around another skill. Review safety immediately and look for less distress across a week rather than waiting for a promised end date.

### Should I put cushions in the cot because baby keeps falling?

No. Pillows, bumpers, nests and soft objects can create suffocation, entrapment or climbing hazards. Keep the sleep space clear and practise controlled lowering while awake.

### Can I hold my baby down so they fall asleep?

No. Do not pin or restrain a baby. Place them on their back to begin sleep, offer calm reassurance and allow self-chosen movement in a safe cot.

### Should the mattress already be on the lowest setting?

Check the cot manual now. Public-health guidance advises lowering it before standing—when baby can sit or push up on hands and knees. If the correct setup is unclear, stop using the cot until the manufacturer can confirm it.

### Does standing at night mean my baby is undertired?

Not by itself. A cheerful baby practising for a long time might also have light sleep pressure, but a brand-new skill can appear even with sensible timing. Compare the final wake window, naps and actual sleep onset across several nights.

## The calm plan in one line

**Lower and clear the cot, teach “bend, lower, sit” during the day, help when baby is genuinely stuck and keep bedtime too boring to become a standing ovation.**
