Your baby feels hot against your chest. The thermometer beeps: 38.1°C. Is that a mild fever to watch, an urgent call, or an emergency?

The number matters—but your baby's **age and condition** matter just as much.

The NHS defines a high temperature in babies and children as **38°C or more**. Call NHS 111 or ask for an urgent GP appointment if a baby under 3 months has a temperature of 38°C or higher, or if a baby aged 3 to 6 months has a temperature of 39°C or higher. Call 999 for serious accompanying signs such as difficulty breathing, blue or grey colour, a non-fading rash, being hard to wake, not responding normally or a first seizure.

> **Quick answer:** take the temperature with a digital thermometer in the armpit. Do not subtract or add degrees. Use your baby's age to choose the right action, then look beyond the number at breathing, colour, alertness, feeding, wet nappies and your instincts.

## Baby fever thresholds at a glance

| Baby's age or situation | Temperature | What NHS guidance says |
|---|---:|---|
| Any baby or child | **38°C or more** | This is a high temperature or fever |
| Under 3 months | **38°C or more**, or you think they have a high temperature | Call NHS 111 or ask for an urgent GP appointment |
| 3 to 6 months | **39°C or more**, or you think they have a high temperature | Call NHS 111 or ask for an urgent GP appointment |
| Any age | Fever lasting **5 days or more** | Call NHS 111 or seek urgent GP advice |
| Under 5 with possible sepsis | **Below 36°C**, feeling cold or shivery, or serious warning signs | Call 999 or go to A&E under current NHS sepsis advice |
| Any age | A worrying child, regardless of the number | Trust your instincts and seek help |

These are action thresholds, not a way to diagnose the cause. A 38.1°C reading in a 6-week-old and the same reading in a lively 10-month-old lead to different decisions. A child can also be seriously unwell without a high temperature.

Use the [current NHS fever guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/fever-in-children/) and contact NHS 111 if you are uncertain. If your baby has an individual plan because they were premature, have a weakened immune system or another medical condition, follow that plan.

## How to take a baby's temperature accurately

For children under 5, the [NHS recommends a digital thermometer in the armpit](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/health/how-to-take-your-babys-temperature/).

### 1. Settle your baby on your lap

Hold them comfortably and uncover one armpit. The skin should be dry. You do not need to remove every item of clothing or make the room cold.

### 2. Put the thermometer high in the armpit

Place the tip against the skin at the top of the armpit—not through clothing and not loosely beside the arm.

### 3. Hold the arm gently against the body

Keep the thermometer in place for the time stated by its instructions, usually until it beeps. Movement or a loose arm can produce an unreliable result.

### 4. Read and record the number as shown

Do not add a degree to an armpit reading. Record the time, method and exact number. If the result is implausible or does not fit how the baby feels, check the positioning and repeat once with the same method.

### 5. Act on an urgent reading rather than repeatedly measuring

If a young baby reaches an age-specific urgent threshold or your child looks seriously unwell, make the call. Ten repeated readings do not make the first concerning one less important.

## When should you wait before checking again?

A reading may be temporarily affected if your baby has just:

- had a warm bath
- been wrapped tightly in a blanket
- been in a very warm room
- worn many layers
- been very active
- cuddled a hot water bottle

Allow them to cool naturally for a few minutes—without making them cold or shivery—then take the temperature again.

Do not delay if your baby seems very unwell, is difficult to wake, has breathing difficulty or has another emergency sign. The instruction to wait applies to a comfortable child whose reading may have been affected by external warmth.

## Which thermometer should you use?

### Best starting choice: a digital underarm thermometer

It is inexpensive, widely available and the NHS-recommended method for children under 5 when used according to its instructions.

### Ear thermometers

Ear thermometers can be quick, but a tiny ear canal makes correct positioning difficult in babies. A poor angle can give a misleading reading. Follow the manufacturer's age guidance and do not rely on an uncertain ear result when a baby looks unwell.

### Forehead strip thermometers

Forehead strips measure skin temperature rather than body temperature and are not an accurate way to check a baby's fever.

### Mercury thermometers

Do not use an old glass thermometer containing mercury. It can break and release poisonous mercury as well as glass.

Whatever device you own, use one reliable method consistently. A trend built from different body sites and devices can look more meaningful than it is.

## The number is only one part of the safety check

A temperature is an observation, not a diagnosis. While you wait for advice or care for a mild illness at home, look at the whole baby.

### Breathing

Are they breathing comfortably? Grunting, very fast breathing, obvious breathlessness or the stomach sucking in under the ribs needs emergency help.

### Colour

Look at lips, tongue, palms and soles as well as the face. Blue, grey, unusually pale or blotchy colour is an emergency sign and may be easier to notice in these areas on brown or black skin.

### Alertness and response

Are they waking, looking at you and responding in their usual way? A baby who is hard to wake, floppy, confused or not responding normally needs urgent help.

### Feeding and wet nappies

Offer breastfeeds as normal and other appropriate fluids. Much less feeding, repeated vomiting, nappies that are not very wet, no tears or sunken eyes can point to dehydration or worsening illness.

### Rash

A rash that does not fade when pressed with a clear glass is an emergency sign. On darker skin, check lighter areas such as the palms and soles too.

### Your instinct

You know what “not themselves” looks like. The [NHS serious-illness guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/health/is-your-baby-or-toddler-seriously-ill/) explicitly tells parents to trust their instincts. You do not need a dramatic number to ask for help.

## What to do at home for a fever

Most fevers in children return to normal within 1 to 4 days, and many can be cared for at home when the child is otherwise well and no urgent age threshold or warning sign applies.

### Do

- offer plenty of fluids
- continue breastfeeding normally and offer feeds frequently
- give food if your child wants it
- check on them regularly, including during the night
- keep them at home
- keep clothing and bedding comfortable rather than excessive
- seek advice if you are worried or they are getting worse

### Do not

- sponge the child with cool water
- undress them completely to force the temperature down
- wrap them in too many clothes or bedclothes
- give aspirin to anyone under 16
- use temperature alone as proof they are getting better

The aim is comfort and hydration, not forcing the thermometer back to 36.4°C.

## Paracetamol and ibuprofen: treat distress, not the display

The NHS says children's paracetamol or ibuprofen may be used if a child is distressed or uncomfortable, provided the medicine is suitable for that child. Check the packaging and leaflet, or ask a pharmacist or GP if you are unsure.

Important restrictions include:

- do not give paracetamol to a baby under 2 months
- do not give ibuprofen to a baby under 3 months or under 5kg
- do not give ibuprofen if the child is dehydrated or has chickenpox
- ask a doctor before ibuprofen if the child has asthma
- do not alternate paracetamol and ibuprofen unless a doctor or nurse tells you to
- never give aspirin to a child under 16

Use the product made for your child's age, measure with the supplied oral syringe and follow the exact label dose and interval. Do not estimate a dose from an adult product or copy another child's plan.

A temperature falling after medicine does not prove the illness is harmless. Equally, the number not returning fully to normal does not by itself identify a dangerous infection. How the child looks, breathes, feeds and responds still matters.

## When to call NHS 111 or seek urgent GP advice

Call NHS 111 or ask for an urgent GP appointment if:

- your baby is under 3 months with 38°C or higher, or you think they have a high temperature
- your baby is 3 to 6 months with 39°C or higher, or you think they have a high temperature
- the child has a rash as well as a high temperature
- fever has lasted 5 days or more
- they do not want to eat, are not their usual self and you are worried
- they show dehydration signs such as less-wet nappies, sunken eyes or no tears
- medicine is not helping their distress
- you are worried for any other reason

You can call before a threshold if the baby looks unwell. The age numbers tell you when not to wait; they do not tell you to ignore everything below them.

## When to call 999

Call 999 if a baby or child:

- has difficulty breathing, grunts, breathes very fast or has the stomach sucking in under the ribs
- has blue, grey, unusually pale or blotchy skin, lips or tongue
- has a rash that does not fade under a clear glass
- is drowsy and hard to wake
- is not responding normally or is not interested in feeding or normal activity
- has a stiff neck or is bothered by light
- is extremely agitated, cannot stop crying or seems confused
- has a seizure for the first time

The [current NHS sepsis page](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/sepsis/) also says to call 999 or go to A&E for a baby or child under 5 with possible sepsis who has a very low temperature below 36°C, feels cold to the touch or is shivery, or has the age-specific very high temperatures listed above alongside concern for sepsis.

Do not wait for a rash. A seriously unwell child may not have every symptom.

## What about fever after vaccinations?

Some babies develop a temperature after routine vaccinations. Follow the aftercare information given at the appointment and the NHS instructions for that vaccine.

Do not automatically assume every fever is “just the jabs”. Contact a health professional if the fever is high for your baby's age, lasts longer than expected, the baby is getting worse or another warning sign appears.

If you were specifically advised to give infant paracetamol after a MenB vaccination, follow the current schedule and dose supplied by the vaccination team or NHS leaflet. That plan does not become a general dosing rule for other illnesses.

## Fever and sleep: what should happen tonight?

A feverish baby may sleep more, wake more, feed differently or need extra comfort. This is illness care, not a night to test a new sleep strategy.

- continue the baby's usual safe sleep setup
- place them on their back in a clear, flat, separate sleep space
- avoid extra blankets or over-wrapping
- check them regularly, including overnight
- offer feeds and comfort responsively
- seek help if they become difficult to wake or their breathing, colour, feeding or nappies concern you

Do not use a sleep sack's TOG chart as fever treatment, and do not wake solely to chase thermometer readings unless a health professional has advised monitoring. Follow the clinical plan you receive.

Once the baby is well, rebuild the familiar routine gently. Our [post-illness sleep reset guide](/blog/baby-sleep-worse-after-illness-reset-plan.html) explains how to unwind temporary extra support without treating comfort during illness as a bad habit.

## A simple fever log that helps a clinician

Record only information you actually know:

| Time | Useful observation |
|---|---|
| Temperature check | Exact number, underarm method and thermometer used |
| Medicine | Name, formulation, dose and time—copied from what was actually given |
| Feeding | Breastfeed, measured bottle or refused feed; do not invent breast-milk ml |
| Nappies | Wet nappy times and any vomiting or diarrhoea |
| Behaviour | Alert, unsettled, unusually sleepy, difficult to wake or not responding normally |
| Breathing and colour | Comfortable or changed; serious change means call 999 rather than documenting longer |

The purpose is not to build a perfect spreadsheet while your baby needs you. Two or three precise entries are more useful than estimated data. Safety action always comes before logging.

## Common temperature mistakes

### “The thermometer says 37.9°C, so nothing can be wrong”

Thresholds do not cancel symptoms. A baby who is floppy, blue, struggling to breathe or hard to wake needs emergency help regardless of the reading.

### “The fever came down, so the illness must be minor”

Medicine can improve comfort and lower temperature in many illnesses. Continue watching the child, not just the display.

### “They feel cool now, so I do not need to check”

Touch is useful for noticing change but cannot replace a thermometer when you need an accurate number. Very low temperature can also be concerning.

### “I should alternate medicines to keep the temperature down”

Do not alternate paracetamol and ibuprofen unless a health professional tells you to. It increases the chance of dose confusion and is not routine NHS advice.

### “I need to undress or sponge the baby”

The NHS advises against undressing or sponging a child to cool them. Keep them comfortably dressed without over-wrapping.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is 37.5°C a fever in a baby?

The NHS defines fever as 38°C or more. However, a baby can be unwell below that number. For a very young baby or any child who is not feeding, unusually sleepy, difficult to wake or otherwise concerning, seek advice based on the whole picture.

### Is 38°C dangerous for a baby?

38°C is the fever line. In a baby under 3 months it requires urgent advice from NHS 111 or a GP, even if the baby otherwise looks well. In an older baby, the number is interpreted alongside age, symptoms and behaviour.

### Is 39°C high for a 4-month-old?

Yes. NHS guidance says a baby aged 3 to 6 months with 39°C or higher needs urgent advice through NHS 111 or an urgent GP appointment.

### What temperature is normal for a baby?

The NHS says normal is about 36.4°C but varies slightly between children. Do not use a narrow “perfect” number as the goal; measurement method and how the baby is behaving matter.

### Can teething cause a high fever?

Do not explain a true fever away as teething. If the temperature is 38°C or more, treat it as fever and follow the age and symptom guidance. Teething and an infection can happen at the same time.

### Should I wake a sleeping baby to give fever medicine?

Use medicine for distress or discomfort and follow the advice given for your child. Do not create your own overnight dosing schedule. If a clinician has told you to give doses at specific times, follow that plan; otherwise ask NHS 111, a GP or pharmacist when unsure.
