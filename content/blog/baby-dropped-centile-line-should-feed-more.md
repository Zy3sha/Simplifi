Your baby's newest dot sits lower on the growth chart. Suddenly every short breastfeed, unfinished bottle and refused spoonful feels like evidence. Should you start offering more milk tonight?

**Do not change your baby's feeding plan from one dot alone.** First check that the measurements were accurate and correctly plotted. Continue feeding responsively, look at your baby's wellbeing and feeding across the whole day, and ask your health visitor or GP to interpret a confirmed centile change. If your baby is feeding poorly, has fewer wet nappies, is unusually sleepy or seems unwell, seek advice promptly rather than waiting for another weigh-in.

A centile crossing is a reason to add context—not a verdict on your feeding.

## What to do next, at a glance

| What you noticed | Sensible next step | What not to conclude |
|---|---|---|
| One unexpected home-scale number | Do not repeatedly reweigh. Record how it was taken and ask whether a clinic measurement is needed. | “My milk supply has suddenly failed.” |
| A correctly plotted move across one centile space | Mention it to your health visitor, especially if feeding, nappies or wellbeing have changed. Follow their monitoring plan. | “I must get my baby back to the old line.” |
| A move across two centile spaces | Contact your health visitor or GP for assessment; this is less common and deserves clinical context. | “More milk is definitely the whole answer.” |
| Current weight below the 2nd centile, or a concern already being monitored | Follow the individual clinical plan and arrange review as advised. | “An app can clear this as normal.” |
| Poor feeding, fewer wet nappies, repeated vomiting, marked sleepiness or an unwell baby | Seek prompt medical advice. Call 111 in the UK when you need urgent guidance; call 999 in an emergency. | “I should wait until the next scheduled weigh-in.” |

These are conversation prompts, not diagnostic cut-offs. NICE's thresholds for concern vary with birthweight: a fall across one or more spaces may matter when birthweight was below the 9th centile, two or more when it was between the 9th and 91st, and three or more when it was above the 91st. Current weight below the 2nd centile is also a threshold. A clinician applies those rules to the professionally plotted history and the child in front of them.

## What does “dropped a centile line” mean?

A centile compares your baby's measurement with the chart reference for children of the same age and sex. It is not a score. A baby on the 25th centile is not doing worse than one on the 75th, and the 50th centile is not a target.

Parents often say “line” when they mean a **centile space**: the area between two printed curves. The NHS explains that a baby's weight and height may not follow one line exactly. Moving by one centile line can happen; crossing two is less common and should be discussed with a health visitor.

The direction only becomes meaningful when the points are comparable. A recent feed, wee, poo, clothing, a different scale or a plotting mistake can change the picture. That is why the answer is rarely to weigh again tomorrow. [Well-spaced, accurate measurements are more useful than frequent weighing](/blog/how-often-should-you-weigh-your-baby.html).

## So, should I feed more?

Keep offering breast or bottle feeds responsively while you arrange the right review. Do not force a baby to finish, add extra formula powder, dilute feeds, introduce high-calorie additions or start routine top-ups purely because an app or one measurement looked low.

“Feed more” can hide several different questions:

- Is milk being offered often enough for this baby's age and cues?
- Is the baby attaching and transferring milk effectively at the breast?
- Is bottle flow, pacing or teat choice making feeds difficult?
- Are vomiting, reflux symptoms, mouth pain, illness or tiredness interrupting feeds?
- Is formula being prepared exactly as directed?
- Has milk intake changed as solids increased?

NICE recommends a detailed feeding history when there is concern about faltering growth and says direct observation of a feed may be helpful. That can reveal more than counting feeds alone. One long breastfeed is not automatically better than three short effective ones, and a feed log cannot measure how much milk a breastfed baby transferred.

If you are worried about milk supply, look for a pattern rather than treating cluster feeding or pump output as proof. Our guides to [cluster feeding versus low milk supply](/blog/cluster-feeding-or-low-milk-supply.html) and [what pump output can—and cannot—tell you](/blog/is-my-pump-output-normal.html) explain the difference.

## Take the useful context, not a perfect diary

You do not need to reconstruct every minute since birth. Bring the few details that help a clinician see what happened between measurements:

- the exact weight values, dates and where each measurement was taken
- whether the same professional baby scales and plotting chart were used
- breastfeeds, expressed milk or formula feeds, including any noticeable change in frequency or ease
- wet nappies and any clear change from your baby's usual pattern
- repeated vomiting, diarrhoea, constipation, fever or a recent illness
- alertness, feeding cues and whether your baby is difficult to wake for feeds
- birth history, prematurity and any existing feeding or growth plan
- for an older baby, how milk feeds changed as solids were introduced

NICE advises measuring length or height as well as weight when there is concern, plotting all available measurements and considering the parents' and baby's interaction around feeding. The purpose is not to grade you. It is to work out whether the plotted change is real and what support—if any—would help.

## Situations that need their own plan

### The first weeks after birth

Early weight loss and regaining birthweight are assessed differently from a later centile drift. Babies are routinely monitored in the first 2 weeks, and most regain birthweight by around 3 weeks. If your midwife or feeding team is monitoring weight loss, jaundice, attachment or milk transfer, follow that individual plan rather than this general guide.

### After illness

Illness can temporarily slow weight gain. The NHS notes that many babies return to their previous pattern within 2 to 3 weeks. That does not mean every drop should be watched without advice: an unwell baby or one feeding poorly needs assessment now. It means the clinician may interpret a post-illness point differently from an unexplained longer trend.

### Prematurity

Growth assessment for a baby born early needs gestational and corrected-age context. Neonatal teams may use specialised charts or plans. A consumer app must never overrule them.

### Starting solids

Solids do not turn a centile into a portion target. Milk remains an important source of nutrition through the first year, while appetite and food exploration naturally vary. Avoid pressuring extra spoonfuls to chase a curve; ask for individual feeding advice if milk intake, swallowing or growth is concerning.

## A calm script for the appointment

You can say:

> “These two weights were taken on these dates and appear to cross this centile space. Feeding changed in this way between them, and wet nappies are the same/different. Could you check the plotting and watch a feed before we decide whether anything needs to change?”

That question invites assessment without assuming either that something is wrong or that everything is fine.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a baby naturally change centiles?

Yes. Measurements do not have to sit precisely on one line, and a move of one centile space can occur. What matters is the size and persistence of the change, starting centile, measurement quality, feeding and the baby's overall health.

### Should I weigh my baby again tomorrow?

Usually not unless a health professional has asked you to. Very close measurements can amplify normal variation and scale error. Ask your health visitor when and where the next weight should be taken.

### Does frequent feeding mean my baby is not getting enough?

Not by itself. Cluster feeding can be normal, especially in younger babies. Effective milk transfer, growth, wet nappies and clinical wellbeing provide more context than feed count alone.

### Can OBubba tell me to top up with formula?

No. It can organise measurements and logged patterns, but it cannot assess a feed or prescribe supplementation. A midwife, health visitor, infant-feeding specialist, GP or paediatric team should make that plan with you.
