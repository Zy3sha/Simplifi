The first day back can feel like you need to solve six months of feeding in advance. You do not. **Make a plan for one ordinary workday:** when your baby can feed with you, who will offer milk while you are apart, where expressed milk will be kept, and how you will hear what actually happened. Then try it once and adjust.

Returning to work does not mean you must stop breastfeeding. You might breastfeed before and after work, express during the day, have your baby brought to you for a feed, or combine breastfeeding with expressed milk or first infant formula. What fits depends on your baby's age, your hours, your body and your childcare. The [NHS outlines these options](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/breastfeeding-and-bottle-feeding/breastfeeding-and-lifestyle/back-to-work/) without prescribing one right timetable.

This is general information, not a personal feeding or employment-rights plan. If your baby is premature, has a feeding plan, or is not gaining weight as expected, check any changes with your health visitor, infant-feeding team or clinician.

## A few days before: rehearse the handover, not a perfect routine

Choose a short practice period with the person who will care for your baby. Write down three things: the last feed, the milk available for the next feed, and how the carer will tell you what your baby took. The [NHS suggests a trial run before returning to work](https://www.nhs.uk/best-start-in-life/baby/feeding-your-baby/breastfeeding/breastfeeding-returning-to-work/). It is useful for learning where the practical snags are, not for testing whether your baby can meet a target number of millilitres.

If your baby has not taken a bottle before, give them and the carer time to learn together while everyone is calm. [NHS mixed-feeding advice](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/breastfeeding-and-bottle-feeding/bottle-feeding/combine-breast-and-bottle/) suggests offering a first bottle when baby is relaxed rather than very hungry, and notes that a baby aged six months or more who can drink milk from a cup may not need a bottle. Our [gentle guide to bottle refusal](/blog/baby-refusing-bottle-gentle-plan.html) has more options; avoid making one difficult practice feed a battle.

If you plan to replace some breastfeeds with formula, do it gradually where possible. The [NHS explains that a gradual change can help avoid painful fullness or mastitis](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/breastfeeding-and-bottle-feeding/bottle-feeding/combine-breast-and-bottle/). Ask a breastfeeding supporter for an individual plan if you are unsure how to change feeds while maintaining the milk supply you want.

## Ask work for the practical things you need

Before returning, tell your employer in writing that you are breastfeeding and ask where you can rest, express privately if you choose, clean equipment, and store milk. Ask how breaks will work in your actual day rather than relying on a vague promise. The [Health and Safety Executive's guidance](https://www.hse.gov.uk/mothers/worker/index.htm) says toilets are not a suitable or hygienic place to express and sets out workplace health-and-safety responsibilities. If arrangements are difficult, speak with HR, your union or an employment adviser rather than trying to improvise a private space on your first day.

There is no single pumping frequency that suits everyone. The [NHS return-to-work guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/best-start-in-life/baby/feeding-your-baby/breastfeeding/breastfeeding-returning-to-work/) says you *might* need to express at least every three hours when first away from your baby. Your baby's usual feeds, your comfort, your workday and any clinical advice matter more than copying another parent's schedule. Seek health advice if a breast becomes swollen, hot and painful, you feel feverish or flu-like, or expressing remains difficult; the [NHS explains the signs of mastitis](https://www.nhs.uk/best-start-in-life/baby/feeding-your-baby/breastfeeding/breastfeeding-challenges/mastitis/).

## Make the milk handover safe and unambiguous

Label and date each container so the carer knows which milk to use. Check that both your workplace and childcare can keep it cold, and agree how it will travel between them. The [NHS expressing-and-storage guide](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/breastfeeding-and-bottle-feeding/breastfeeding/expressing-breast-milk/) gives the current fridge, freezer and cool-bag limits; follow those instructions rather than a timing suggested by a tracking app. It also advises small quantities to avoid waste. Once your baby has drunk from a bottle of breast milk, use it within one hour and discard what remains.

For the carer, one line is enough: *“Milk is labelled by date; please tell me when a feed started and roughly how much baby actually drank.”* They should feed responsively, holding your baby and allowing pauses, not press them to finish a prepared amount. If a baby repeatedly takes much less than usual, has fewer wet nappies, seems unwell or you have any concern about hydration or growth, contact your health visitor or GP.

## The first week: review the day, not every ounce

At pickup, ask what your baby **drank**, not just what was offered. A simple handover might be: *“Breastfed before drop-off; carer offered two bottles; baby drank one and a half; breastfed after pickup.”* Expressing during the workday is a separate event, not another feed your baby received. Our [combination-feeding record guide](/blog/combination-feeding-without-counting-milk-twice.html) explains that distinction.

OBubba's **Track** screen has Breast, Feed and Pump logs. You can use them to remember the last breastfeed, a bottle your baby drank and a pumping session without treating expressed milk as baby intake twice. Keep the record useful to you and your carer; it is not a score of your supply or your baby's appetite. The [carer-handover checklist](/blog/baby-care-handover-template-grandparents-nursery.html) can help with the other essentials, such as naps and contact details.

After two or three workdays, ask a small question: *What actually needs changing?* Perhaps the carer needs smaller labelled portions, you need a more realistic break, or your baby prefers to feed more when you reunite. You can adjust one thing at a time. If breastfeeding or expressing hurts, milk supply worries are consuming the day, or feeding is not going well, ask your health visitor, midwife or a breastfeeding supporter for help. The [National Breastfeeding Helpline](https://www.nhs.uk/best-start-in-life/baby/feeding-your-baby/breastfeeding/expressing-your-breast-milk/) is also available around the clock.

You are allowed to feel relieved, sad, proud and unsure in the same week. If one shared place for feeds and handovers would lighten the mental load, you can [explore OBubba](/app.html) when you are ready.
