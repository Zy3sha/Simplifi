Every baby sleep app now promises to predict the next nap, but almost nobody explains *how* it gets to that time, or why the prediction is often better than the age chart you could look up for free. The short version: good nap prediction starts from an age-appropriate wake window, then **adapts to your baby's own logged sleep** so the timing gets more personal the longer you use it. The accuracy comes from the learning, not the chart.

Here is what actually happens under the hood, and why a generic chart on its own keeps missing.

## Step 1: the age-appropriate starting point

On day one, an app has nothing personal to go on, so it starts where everyone starts: a typical wake window for your baby's age. Our [wake windows by age chart](/blog/wake-windows-by-age.html) is that starting point, the broad ranges a newborn, a six-month-old or a toddler tends to manage between sleeps. This is the same information a printed chart gives you, and on its own it is only a rough guide.

## Step 2: it learns your baby

This is where prediction pulls ahead of a chart. As you log naps, night sleep and wake-ups, the timing shifts toward *your* baby:

- **Your baby's real wake windows**, which are often 30 to 45 minutes different from the textbook, and different in the morning versus the evening.
- **How the windows are stretching** as your baby grows, so the prediction moves with them instead of staying frozen on a chart.
- **What the actual day did**, so a short nap, a late start or a missed nap changes the next suggested time rather than being ignored.

A chart cannot do any of this, because a chart does not know your baby. It prints the average and stops. The prediction keeps adjusting.

## Step 3: it adapts when the day goes sideways

Real days do not follow the plan. A nap runs 25 minutes, the morning starts an hour early, a car ride steals a nap. Good prediction recalculates from what *actually* happened, not from what the schedule hoped for. That is the difference between a countdown timer set to a fixed number and a prediction that reflects the real day.

## Why generic charts fail on their own

Age charts are a fine orientation, but as a standalone tool they fail for predictable reasons:

1. **They assume an average baby.** Two healthy babies the same age can want their naps 30 to 45 minutes apart.
2. **They freeze in time.** A baby's windows lengthen around growth spurts, [developmental leaps](/blog/4-month-sleep-regression.html) and each nap transition; a printed number does not.
3. **They ignore today.** A chart says "2 hours" whether the last nap was a full hour or a 20-minute catnap, and those two days need very different next-nap timing.
4. **They do not explain.** When the nap fails, a chart cannot tell you it was a too-long wake window versus a too-short one, so you cannot learn from it.

This is exactly why a [short nap](/blog/baby-only-naps-30-minutes-short-naps.html) or a rough night so often traces back to timing that a fixed chart could never have caught.

## What makes a nap prediction actually good

Not all predictions are equal. The ones worth trusting:

- **Learn quickly from a few days of real logs**, not just an install-day guess.
- **Keep adapting** as your baby grows and through nap transitions.
- **Explain the why**, so a missed nap teaches you something.
- **Move bedtime with the day**, because the naps and the night are one system.

Be wary of any app leading with a single headline accuracy percentage. Prediction quality depends on your baby and how much you have logged, so a fixed "X% accurate" claim is marketing, not a promise for your baby. The honest test is whether the predictions line up with *your* child once you have logged a few days.
