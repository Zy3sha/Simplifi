The first week of solids can produce an astonishing amount of data from a very small spoon. How much went in? Did the broccoli count if it mostly reached the floor? Was that face dislike, surprise or gagging? Should you record milk as well?

**A useful weaning log does not need every bite. Track what was offered, its important ingredients and texture, whether it contained a new allergen, and any clear response. Keep milk feeds visible too.** Over time, those simple entries can reveal variety, repeated refusals, texture progress, possible reaction timing and whether solids are beginning to crowd out milk.

At the start, eating quantity matters less than learning. The NHS says babies will still get most of their energy and nutrients from breast milk or first infant formula, and that parents should trust them to eat as much or as little as they want.

> **Record the meal well enough to remember it—not so precisely that feeding your baby becomes a measurement exercise.**

## The five things worth recording

For most families, one solids entry can stay this small:

1. **What you offered.** Use a recognisable name such as “lentil and carrot mash”, not “lunch”.
2. **The texture or serving form.** Smooth, fork-mashed, soft finger food, minced or family-food pieces tells you more than grams.
3. **Important ingredients.** Name a newly introduced allergen and anything you may need to identify later.
4. **Your baby's response.** Loved, neutral, refused, gagged, vomited or possible reaction—use plain observations rather than a diagnosis.
5. **When it happened.** A date and approximate time are usually enough; accurate timing becomes especially useful if symptoms follow.

A practical entry might be:

> **08:20 — Porridge with pear and smooth peanut butter, thick mashed texture. First peanut. Ate a few spoonfuls, then turned away. No symptoms noticed.**

That captures the food, form, new allergen, response and timing. It does not pretend to know the exact amount swallowed.

## What not to turn into a daily target

You usually do not need to count:

- spoonfuls, licks or grams at every meal
- a clean-plate percentage
- how many foods touched the floor
- calories or macronutrients unless a clinician asks you to
- a perfect daily total of unique foods
- a streak that must never be broken

The NHS says that at the beginning, how much a baby eats is less important than getting used to eating. Babies may need a new food or texture offered many times before accepting it. A refusal is information, not failure.

If growth, swallowing, allergy, prematurity or a medical condition requires a quantified record, follow the format your health visitor, GP, paediatrician, dietitian or feeding team requests. That is a different job from an everyday family log.

## A calm starting-solids log

| Useful field | A good entry | Why it helps |
|---|---|---|
| food | lentil and carrot mash | shows variety and iron-containing foods more clearly than “dinner” |
| texture | fork-mashed with soft lumps | helps you notice whether textures are becoming more diverse |
| allergen | first well-cooked egg | separates a new introduction from an established ingredient |
| response | touched, tasted, swallowed some, turned away | respects appetite without scoring the baby |
| symptom | raised rash around mouth 10 minutes later; photo taken | gives a health professional timing and a concrete description |
| context | recovering from a cold; unusually tired | stops one difficult meal becoming a verdict on the food |
| milk | usual breastfeeds; 3 formula bottles logged | keeps the main first-year drink visible alongside solids |

Do not add every field to every meal. Context is useful when it may explain an unusual day; it is clutter when nothing notable happened.

## Track variety across a week, not pressure within a meal

UK guidance recommends a wide variety of foods, including iron-containing foods, in age-appropriate forms from around six months. Flavours and textures should become increasingly diverse as complementary feeding develops.

The word **variety** can become unhelpful if it turns into a race to 100 foods. Instead, look back over a week or two:

- were vegetables, fruit, starchy foods and protein foods offered?
- did an iron-containing food appear regularly?
- did baby meet more than one flavour and texture?
- were familiar foods repeated as well as new foods introduced?
- is one easy favourite accidentally replacing every other opportunity?

A food does not need to be accepted to count as an offer. Repeated, pressure-free exposure is part of learning. Keep a short list of foods offered and let the pattern—not a daily score—prompt the next meal.

## Make iron visible

From around six months, include foods that provide iron. NHS examples include meat, fish, fortified breakfast cereals, dark green vegetables, beans and lentils; eggs and tofu can also contribute within a varied diet.

You do not need to calculate milligrams at home. A simpler record is “iron-containing meal offered”. Examples include:

- lentil dhal with a suitable texture
- softly cooked beans mashed into vegetables
- well-cooked egg
- soft flakes of cooked, boneless fish
- minced meat in a soft sauce
- iron-fortified baby cereal or porridge
- tofu prepared in an age-appropriate form

One label cannot assess the whole nutritional quality of a meal, and plant iron is absorbed differently from haem iron. If your baby eats a vegan or restricted diet, has poor growth, was premature or has a known deficiency, get individual dietetic advice rather than relying on an app counter.

## Record texture as an opportunity, not a level passed

Texture matters because babies are learning to move food around the mouth, chew and swallow. The NHS advises moving towards mashed and finger foods as soon as a baby is ready and continuing to offer lumpy textures from around six to seven months.

Useful texture notes include:

- smooth purée
- thick purée
- fork-mashed with soft lumps
- soft finger-sized piece
- minced or finely chopped family food
- mixed texture, such as pasta in sauce

Do not use the log to force a baby up a ladder on a birthday. Development varies, and babies can eat a mixture of textures in the same week. If your baby repeatedly coughs, chokes, seems distressed, cannot move beyond smooth foods, has a wet or gurgly voice after eating, or loses skills, seek advice from a health visitor, GP or feeding and swallowing professional.

Gagging can be a normal protective response while learning, but choking is an emergency. Learn the difference and infant first aid before you need it; our [gagging versus choking guide](/blog/gagging-vs-choking-baby-weaning.html) is a starting point, not a substitute for training.

## Give allergens their own clear record

When introducing foods that commonly cause allergy from around six months, the NHS recommends introducing them one at a time so a reaction can be identified. Once tolerated, they should become part of the baby's usual diet.

For a first introduction, record:

- the specific allergenic food, not simply “toast” or “breakfast”
- the other ingredients served with it
- the approximate time
- the form used, such as well-cooked egg or smooth peanut butter thinned into porridge
- what your baby actually did: licked, tasted or ate some
- any symptom, when it began and how it changed

Do not give whole nuts to a baby, and do not offer a sticky lump of nut butter. If your baby has eczema, an existing food allergy or another higher-risk situation, speak to your GP or health visitor before introduction.

Possible allergy symptoms can include swelling, a raised itchy rash, vomiting, coughing, wheezing or breathing changes. Stop the suspected food and seek medical advice. Call **999** for breathing difficulty, swelling of the tongue or throat, collapse, marked floppiness or another sign of anaphylaxis. Do not use a tracker to decide that a severe reaction can wait.

## Keep milk in the same picture

Breast milk or first infant formula remains a baby's main drink throughout the first year. Early solids complement milk; they do not immediately replace it.

Recording both can help answer a more useful question than “how many spoonfuls?”: **is the overall feeding pattern still responsive and appropriate for this stage?**

Look for changes over several days, not one unusual afternoon. A distracted milk feed, a refused lunch or an extra breastfeed during illness can all be ordinary. Seek advice if your baby consistently drinks much less, has fewer wet nappies, appears dehydrated, is unusually sleepy, vomits repeatedly or you are worried about growth.

Never reduce milk deliberately just to make a baby eat more solids. Follow hunger and fullness cues and your clinician's advice.

## A two-minute weekly review

Once a week, ask:

1. **Variety:** what foods and food groups appeared?
2. **Iron:** were iron-containing foods offered?
3. **Texture:** what safe textures did baby explore?
4. **Allergens:** which were new, which tolerated foods were repeated, and was anything uncertain?
5. **Milk and wellbeing:** are feeds, wet nappies, energy and growth broadly reassuring?
6. **Next step:** what is one realistic food or texture to offer next week?

Choose one next step, not six. It might be “offer lentils twice”, “try a fork-mashed texture again” or “write the ingredients rather than logging ‘family dinner’”.

## When a food log becomes evidence for an appointment

A concise record can help a clinician when it shows dates, timing and concrete observations. Bring it if you are worried about:

- possible allergic reactions
- repeated vomiting, diarrhoea, constipation or blood in stools
- persistent coughing, choking or swallowing difficulty
- a very narrow range of accepted foods
- solids repeatedly displacing milk too quickly
- poor weight gain, weight loss or dehydration
- distress, pain or worsening eczema linked with particular foods

Photographs may help with a visible rash if it is safe to take them. Keep packaging or ingredient lists when a mixed product may be involved. Do not delay urgent care while making the diary perfect.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need to track how much my baby eats?

Not routinely. Early in weaning, learning and exposure matter more than precise intake. Record an approximate response—tasted, ate some, refused—unless a clinician has asked for quantities.

### Does food thrown on the floor count?

It counts as an offered food and a learning experience, but not necessarily as eaten. Record “offered; mostly explored or dropped” if the distinction matters.

### Should I count every ingredient as a unique food?

Use whichever system remains consistent and useful. For reactions, name all relevant ingredients. For variety, ingredient-level tracking is more informative than giving every recipe a completely new name, but it does not need to become exhaustive.

### What should I write after gagging?

Record the food, texture, what you saw and how quickly baby recovered. Do not label a choking event as gagging. If breathing was affected or you had to intervene, seek appropriate medical advice and review infant first aid.

### Can an app tell me when my baby is ready for the next texture?

No. Age can provide a prompt, but readiness depends on development and feeding skills. Persistent difficulty deserves assessment rather than another automated level.

### How long should I keep a weaning diary?

Keep it while it reduces uncertainty or supports a clinical question. Once family meals feel ordinary, a full entry for every meal may no longer be useful. Continue preserving allergy and reaction information that could matter later.
