You can love your baby and still feel frightened, flat, angry, numb or unlike yourself. You can be grateful and desperate for a break. You can look as though you are coping while counting the minutes until somebody else walks through the door.

The **baby blues** are short-lived tearfulness, anxiety, irritability or mood swings that commonly begin in the first days after birth and usually settle within two weeks. **Postnatal depression (PND)** is more persistent: symptoms may continue beyond two weeks, become worse, start later or make everyday life difficult. It can begin during pregnancy, soon after birth or at any time in the first year.

Neither is a verdict on how much you love your baby. PND is a common health condition, it is treatable, and asking for help is not a safeguarding confession.

> You do not need to wait for two weeks if you feel unsafe, cannot cope or know that something is seriously wrong.

## If you need help now

**Call 999 or go to A&E now** if you think you may harm yourself or your baby, someone is in immediate danger, or you cannot keep yourself safe.

**Seek an urgent same-day assessment** for sudden confusion, hallucinations, unusual beliefs, extreme agitation, a very high or rapidly changing mood, racing thoughts, or not sleeping at all even when given the chance—especially in the first weeks after birth. These can be signs of postpartum psychosis, which is a medical emergency. Contact your GP immediately; call NHS 111 if you cannot reach them or do not know what to do next.

For someone to listen without judgement, call **Samaritans free on 116 123**, day or night. Samaritans is a listening service, not a substitute for 999 when there is immediate danger.

## Baby blues vs postnatal depression

| | Baby blues | Postnatal depression |
|---|---|---|
| When it appears | Usually in the first days after birth | During pregnancy, soon after birth or any time in the first year |
| How long it lasts | Usually eases within 2 weeks | Persists beyond 2 weeks, starts later or worsens |
| What it may feel like | Tearful, emotional, irritable, anxious or overwhelmed | Persistent low mood, hopelessness, guilt, anxiety, irritability, loss of enjoyment or difficulty coping |
| Impact | Uncomfortable but gradually lifting | Affects daily life, relationships, rest, concentration or connection with baby |
| What to do | Rest, accept practical support and keep talking; ask for help sooner if worried | Tell your GP, midwife or health visitor; treatment and specialist support are available |

The two-week point is a guide, not an endurance target. Severity matters as much as duration. If symptoms are intense on day five, you deserve help on day five.

## What do the baby blues feel like?

Hormonal changes, physical recovery, broken sleep and the shock of responsibility can make the first days emotionally raw. You might:

- cry without knowing why
- feel anxious, touchy or overwhelmed
- swing quickly between joy and sadness
- feel more vulnerable than usual
- struggle with visitors, decisions or small setbacks
- wonder why you do not feel how you expected to feel

Baby blues usually improve within two weeks. During that time, reduce the performance of being “fine.” Let someone bring food, hold the baby while you shower, cancel visitors and listen without solving.

Contact your midwife, health visitor or GP if the feelings are severe, are not beginning to lift, or simply worry you. You do not have to self-diagnose before calling.

## Signs of postnatal depression

PND does not always look like crying. The NHS lists symptoms including:

- low mood or feeling persistently sad
- finding it difficult to enjoy anything
- hopelessness or feeling unable to cope
- guilt, worthlessness or feeling like a “bad parent”
- constant worry, fear, restlessness or irritability
- difficulty sleeping even when there is a chance to rest
- difficulty concentrating or making decisions
- withdrawing from other people
- difficult feelings about, or trouble bonding with, the baby
- thoughts of suicide or harming yourself or the baby

Some parents describe numbness rather than sadness. Others function all day and unravel at night. Anxiety, panic, rage and intrusive thoughts can sit alongside depression.

Having one symptom does not prove PND. But you do not need every symptom before speaking to somebody. The NHS advises contacting a GP, midwife or health visitor if you think you may have postnatal depression, because treatment improves recovery.

## What about frightening intrusive thoughts?

Unwanted, upsetting thoughts can be terrifying precisely because they are the opposite of what you want. They are worth telling a health professional about, even when you have no intention of acting on them. A GP, midwife, health visitor or perinatal mental-health professional can assess what the thoughts mean and help you feel safer.

If the thoughts feel like an urge, a plan or something you may act on—or you cannot confidently keep yourself or the baby safe—call 999 or go to A&E now. Put the baby in a safe place and bring another trusted adult in immediately if possible.

## Postpartum psychosis is different and urgent

Postpartum psychosis is not an intense version of the baby blues. It is a serious illness that often begins suddenly in the first two weeks after birth and can worsen quickly.

Warning signs can include:

- hearing, seeing, smelling or feeling things that are not there
- fixed fears, suspicions or beliefs that are unlikely to be true
- severe confusion or behaviour that is very unlike the person
- feeling extremely high, overactive or unable to slow thoughts and speech
- rapidly changing between high and low moods
- agitation or being unable to sleep at all despite having the opportunity

The person experiencing it may not realise they are unwell. A partner or relative may need to act. Request an urgent same-day assessment from the GP; call NHS 111 if you cannot reach one. Call 999 or go to A&E if there is imminent danger.

With prompt treatment, most people recover. Getting urgent help protects the parent–baby relationship; it does not erase it.

## Broken sleep can blur the picture

Almost every new parent is tired. Sleep deprivation can make anyone more tearful, irritable, forgetful and anxious. That overlap can make it easy to dismiss depression as “just the baby.”

Useful questions are:

- Do I feel any relief after a protected block of rest?
- Can I enjoy or look forward to anything at all?
- Do I feel low, frightened or detached most days?
- Can I sleep when the baby is safely cared for and I have the chance?
- Are guilt, hopelessness or frightening thoughts becoming louder?
- Is this affecting basic care, eating, washing, leaving the house or talking to people?

A night of sleep is support, not a diagnostic test. PND can remain after rest, and severe sleep loss deserves practical and medical attention in its own right.

## The message you can send when words are hard

You do not need a polished explanation. Copy one of these:

> “I had a baby recently and I have felt low and anxious most days. It has lasted more than two weeks and I am struggling to cope. I need an appointment to talk about postnatal mental health.”

Or:

> “I am not sure whether this is postnatal depression, but I do not feel like myself and I need help. Can you stay with me while I call my GP or health visitor?”

For urgent symptoms:

> “This changed suddenly. They are confused/not sleeping/hearing or believing things that are not real. They had a baby recently and need an urgent assessment today.”

If speaking feels impossible, show somebody this paragraph or take a trusted person to the appointment.

## What help might look like

Treatment depends on symptoms, preferences and clinical assessment. NHS options include:

- support from a GP, midwife or health visitor
- NHS talking therapies, which adults can often self-refer to
- cognitive behavioural therapy or another talking therapy
- antidepressant medication
- a specialist community perinatal mental-health team
- a mother and baby unit when hospital treatment is needed for a severe condition

Many antidepressants can be used while breastfeeding; tell the prescriber how you feed so they can choose appropriately. Do not stop prescribed medication suddenly without medical advice.

Practical support matters alongside treatment. A specific ask is easier to act on than “let me know if you need anything”:

- take the 8pm–midnight baby shift
- bring one meal and put it in the fridge
- walk with me to the GP
- hold the baby while I sleep for 90 minutes
- cancel visitors and answer messages for me
- check in tomorrow even if I say I am fine

## Partners and non-birthing parents can struggle too

Postnatal depression can affect fathers and partners. They may show low mood, withdrawal, irritability, anxiety, sleep problems, increased drinking or feeling disconnected from family life.

The hormonal baby blues specifically follow birth, but partners do not need a special label before asking for help. A GP and NHS talking therapies can support them too. In an immediate mental-health emergency, the same 999 rule applies.

## A note for the person reading beside them

Do not debate whether they “really” have depression. Ask directly and calmly:

- “Are you having thoughts of harming yourself or the baby?”
- “What is making it hard to stay safe right now?”
- “Have you heard or seen things other people do not, or felt very confused?”
- “Would you like me to make the call while I sit with you?”

Asking about suicide does not plant the idea. If there is immediate danger, call 999. If postpartum psychosis is possible, seek a same-day urgent assessment even if the person wants to wait.

Then do the practical work: take the baby safely, reduce decisions, bring food and water, write down symptoms and timings, and stay until professional support is in motion.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long do the baby blues last?

They usually settle within two weeks after birth. Ask for help sooner if symptoms are severe, worsening or frightening. If they continue beyond two weeks or start later, speak to your GP, midwife or health visitor.

### Can postnatal depression start months after birth?

Yes. NHS guidance says symptoms can begin during pregnancy, soon after birth or up to a year after the baby is born.

### Can I have postnatal depression if I love and bond with my baby?

Yes. Difficulty bonding can be one symptom, but it is not required. Feeling loving or having good moments does not cancel persistent depression or anxiety.

### Will my baby be taken away if I tell my GP?

The NHS says this is very rare. The purpose of asking is to get support that helps you and your baby stay together and well.

### Is the EPDS a diagnosis?

No. It is a screening questionnaire that can identify answers worth discussing with a health professional. A score does not make or rule out a diagnosis, and urgent safety concerns matter regardless of the total.

### What number should I call in the UK?

Call 999 or go to A&E for immediate danger. Contact a GP immediately for possible postpartum psychosis, or NHS 111 if you cannot reach one. Samaritans is available free on 116 123 for confidential listening at any time.
