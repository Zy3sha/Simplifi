Is this one a contraction? Should you start timing now? Does “five minutes apart” mean leave immediately—or wait until the pattern has lasted an hour?

Near the end of pregnancy, those questions can turn every tightening into a calculation. A contraction timer can hold the numbers for you, but it cannot tell whether your cervix is changing, check your baby or replace the plan from your maternity team.

The short version is:

- start timing when tightenings become noticeable enough that you want to see whether a pattern is forming
- measure each contraction from the moment it starts until it eases
- measure the interval from the **start of one contraction to the start of the next**
- call your midwife or maternity unit if you think labour may have started, your contractions are regular and about five minutes apart, or you are unsure
- follow your own maternity team's instructions if they differ from a general timing rule
- call urgently for waters breaking, bleeding, reduced baby movements, possible labour before 37 weeks, unusually long or very frequent contractions, or any feeling that something is wrong

The timer is a notebook, not a diagnosis. How you feel matters as much as the arithmetic.

## What does a contraction feel like?

A contraction happens when the muscles of the womb tighten and then relax. The sensation is different from person to person and can change throughout labour.

You might notice:

- your bump becoming firm or hard
- period-like cramping
- pressure low in your pelvis
- an ache that begins in your back and moves around the front
- a wave that rises, peaks and fades
- needing to pause, breathe or concentrate until it passes

Some contractions are uncomfortable rather than painful. Some people feel powerful back pressure. Others are not sure anything is happening until the pattern becomes regular.

The [NHS guide to signs of labour](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/labour-and-birth/signs-that-labour-has-begun/) explains that a contraction makes the womb tighten and the discomfort usually builds as it progresses. But sensation alone cannot confirm how far labour has progressed.

## Braxton Hicks or labour contractions?

Braxton Hicks are often called practice contractions. They can happen for weeks before labour and may feel surprisingly strong. Labour contractions are more likely to form a pattern and become longer, stronger or closer together over time.

| What you notice | Braxton Hicks may | Labour contractions may |
|---|---|---|
| Pattern | stay irregular | become increasingly regular |
| Strength | remain similar or settle | build in intensity |
| Length | be brief or inconsistent | become longer and more consistent |
| Movement or rest | sometimes ease after changing activity | continue despite changing position |
| Effect on you | allow easy conversation | increasingly demand your attention |

This is a comparison, not a home test. Painful contractions can happen before established labour, and labour does not always follow a textbook pattern. The only way to know what your cervix is doing is through appropriate assessment.

Call your maternity team if you are unsure. You do not need to correctly label a contraction before asking for advice.

## When should you start timing contractions?

You do not need to record every tightening from the first faint sensation. Start when:

- the contractions are noticeable enough to interrupt what you are doing
- several have happened and you wonder whether they are becoming regular
- they appear to be getting stronger or closer together
- your midwife has asked you to monitor the pattern
- having the information would make a phone conversation with maternity triage easier

Time a small run and then look at the direction of travel. The goal is not to spend the whole latent phase staring at a stopwatch.

If timing makes you anxious, stop. Put your phone down, breathe and notice how you feel. You can call your maternity unit based on your symptoms or instinct without presenting a perfect chart.

## How to time a contraction accurately

For each contraction:

1. Tap **start** when the tightening or wave begins.
2. Tap **stop** when it has fully eased.
3. Record how long that contraction lasted.
4. Measure how far apart contractions are from the **start of one to the start of the next**.

Imagine one contraction starts at 8:00 pm and ends at 8:00:50. The next begins at 8:05 pm.

- duration: 50 seconds
- interval: 5 minutes, measured start to start
- rest between them: 4 minutes 10 seconds

People often accidentally measure from the end of one contraction to the start of the next. That is the resting gap, not the interval usually discussed by maternity teams.

You do not need second-perfect taps. A timer is there to reveal the overall rhythm, not grade your reflexes.

## What does the 5-1-1 rule mean?

The shorthand usually means contractions are:

- about **5 minutes apart**
- lasting about **1 minute**
- continuing in that pattern for roughly **1 hour**

Some maternity services use this as a cue for people having a first baby to call or prepare to come in. It is not a universal law and it is not a reason to ignore other symptoms until an hour has passed.

The NHS says to call for guidance if you think you are in labour or contractions are regular and coming every five minutes or more often. Your team may advise you differently based on:

- whether this is your first birth
- how quickly a previous labour progressed
- the distance to your birth setting
- a planned home birth
- a caesarean scar or other individual clinical factors
- induction or other care already underway
- your baby's position and your wider birth plan

If your own notes say to call at a different point, follow those instructions. When uncertain, call sooner rather than trying to make your body fit an app rule.

## When should you call your maternity unit?

Call for guidance when:

- you think labour may have started
- contractions are regular and about five minutes apart or more frequent
- contractions are becoming stronger and you need support or pain relief
- you are finding it hard to cope at home
- your previous labour was fast
- you live a long way from your planned place of birth
- your maternity team told you to call at a particular point
- you are worried, unsure or simply feel something is not right

The latest [NICE intrapartum-care guidance](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng235/chapter/Recommendations) says maternity triage should ask how you are, discuss your concerns and preferences, ask about baby movements, and agree what should happen next. Calling is part of the care pathway; it is not wasting anyone's time.

## Reasons to call urgently—whatever the timer says

Contact your midwife or maternity unit urgently if:

- your waters break
- you have vaginal bleeding rather than a small blood-streaked show
- your baby is moving less than usual or their pattern changes
- you are under 37 weeks and think labour might be starting
- any contraction lasts longer than two minutes
- you have six or more contractions in ten minutes
- the fluid from your waters is coloured, smelly or heavily bloodstained
- you have constant severe pain between contractions
- you feel unwell, faint, feverish or seriously concerned

Do not wait until the next day, do not wait for a 5-1-1 pattern and do not use a reassuring-looking timer history to delay calling.

The NHS advises calling 111 if you cannot contact your midwife or maternity unit. Call 999 if you think the baby is coming now and you have a strong urge to push. Follow any emergency instructions your maternity team has given you.

Reduced movement always deserves its own immediate call. Read [Baby Movements in Pregnancy: When to Call](/blog/baby-movements-in-pregnancy-when-to-call.html) rather than waiting to see whether contractions become regular.

## What if your waters break before contractions begin?

Waters can release as a gush or a slow trickle that you cannot control. If you think yours have broken:

- note the time
- use a period or maternity pad, not a tampon
- notice the fluid's colour and smell
- call your midwife or maternity unit

Do not wait for a contraction pattern before calling. Tell them immediately if the fluid is green, brown, smelly or accompanied by bleeding, if you feel unwell, or if your baby is moving less.

The team will tell you what assessment and timing applies to your circumstances. Labour often begins after waters break, but infection risk changes once the amniotic sac has opened, so this is not something an app should manage alone.

## What happens when you call maternity triage?

Keep your maternity notes and unit number nearby if possible. The midwife may ask:

- how many weeks pregnant you are
- whether this is your first birth
- when contractions started
- how long they last and how far apart they are
- whether they are becoming stronger
- whether your waters have broken
- whether there is any bleeding
- how your baby is moving
- whether you can talk, walk and cope through each contraction
- your individual pregnancy history and birth plan

They may advise you to stay at home a little longer, attend for assessment or call emergency services. Advice can change if symptoms change, so call again even if you were previously told to remain at home.

NICE describes the latent phase as a period—sometimes not continuous—with contractions and cervical change up to 4 cm. It can last hours or sometimes days. If you attend before established labour and you and your baby are well, you may be supported to return home. That does not mean the contractions were imaginary or that calling was wrong.

## Looking after yourself in early labour

If your maternity team is happy for you to remain at home, simple comfort can help:

- drink regularly and have light food if you feel able
- empty your bladder often
- rest between waves instead of timing continuously
- try a warm bath or shower if your waters and care plan allow
- change position, walk gently or lean forward
- dim the room and reduce unnecessary conversation
- use slow breathing, relaxing your jaw and shoulders as each wave peaks
- let your birth companion manage messages, bags and practical decisions

Ask your maternity team what pain relief is appropriate for you. Individual advice matters, especially after waters break, during induction, with a higher-risk pregnancy or when you have been given a specific plan.

## A useful job for the birth partner

The person supporting you can take over the mechanics:

1. Keep the maternity unit number and notes ready.
2. Start and stop the timer only when asked.
3. Watch the overall pattern instead of announcing every second.
4. Offer water and remind you to release your shoulders.
5. Notice changes in coping, bleeding, waters or baby movements.
6. Call the unit if you ask—or if something concerns them.

Their job is not to persuade you to wait until a target appears. “The app says not yet” is never an appropriate answer to “I need help.”

## A two-line plan to save before labour

Before your due window, write this somewhere easy to find:

> **Call my maternity unit when:** [insert the advice from my care team]
>
> **Call urgently regardless of timing for:** waters, bleeding, reduced movements, labour before 37 weeks, unusually long or frequent contractions, or anything that feels wrong.

Add the correct local number to your phone and your birth partner's phone. General internet guidance cannot know your journey time, previous labour, medical history or local service arrangements.

## The bottom line

Start timing when contractions become noticeable and you want to see whether a rhythm is forming. Measure duration from start to finish and spacing from start to start. A regular five-minute pattern is a common reason to call—but it is not a gate you must pass before receiving care.

Call sooner for urgent symptoms, individual risk factors, difficulty coping or instinct. A good contraction timer holds the arithmetic. You and your maternity team make the decisions.
