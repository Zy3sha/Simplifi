It is 3am. Your baby has fed with their eyes closed and is almost asleep on your shoulder. The nappy line has changed colour—but there is no leak, no smell and no obvious discomfort. Do you wake them fully to change it?

**The short answer:** check at a night feed, but an older baby may not need an automatic change for a mildly wet, absorbent nappy if they are comfortable, their skin is healthy and the nappy is not full or leaking. **Always change a poo or soiled nappy promptly.** Change a wet nappy when it is very full, leaking, uncomfortable, contributing to sore skin or part of a newborn or clinical monitoring plan.

UK guidance generally advises changing wet and dirty nappies promptly to reduce nappy rash. Australian government night-feeding guidance adds a useful practical distinction: a baby may not need a night change unless they seem uncomfortable or have done a poo. Age, skin, nappy fit and the reason for monitoring all matter.

> **Protect the baby, not a perfect sleep streak. A necessary change is care—not a failed night.**

## The 10-second night-change decision

| What you find | What to do |
|---|---|
| poo or a dirty/soiled nappy | change promptly, even if the baby is sleepy |
| leaking, very full or sagging nappy | change; wet clothing and bedding need changing too |
| baby is uncomfortable, repeatedly wriggling or skin looks sore | change, clean gently and review the skin in good light |
| newborn output is being counted, or your midwife/doctor gave a feeding or hydration plan | check and record as instructed; the clinical plan outranks sleep convenience |
| mildly wet nappy, older baby comfortable, skin healthy, no leak | an immediate change may not be necessary; recheck at the next wake or feed |
| you cannot tell whether it is poo | check properly—do not rely only on a wetness line |
| much less urine than usual, very dry nappies or baby seems unwell | stop treating this as a sleep question and seek clinical advice |

A wetness indicator cannot tell you how much urine is present, whether there is poo against the skin or how the skin feels. Use your senses and your baby's behaviour; do not make the line the decision-maker.

## Why poo and wee are different overnight

Urine and faeces both contribute to nappy rash, which is why leaving a baby in a wet or dirty nappy for a long time is not a good plan. But poo is the clearer “change now” signal: faeces contain digestive enzymes and bacteria, and prolonged contact can irritate skin quickly.

A lightly wet modern disposable nappy may pull moisture away from the surface, especially when it fits well. That does not make every wet nappy harmless or every baby the same. Change sooner when:

- the nappy feels heavy, swollen or cool against the skin
- urine is escaping at the legs, back or waist
- the baby already has nappy rash, broken skin or prescribed treatment
- the nappy is rubbing, leaving persistent marks or fitting poorly
- your baby is premature, unwell or on an individual care plan
- you need accurate output information during the early newborn period

Do not leave a soiled nappy because “they might wake”. A short wake is preferable to prolonged contact with poo.

## Newborns need a different threshold

In the first weeks, nappies are not only a hygiene task. They are useful evidence that milk is going in and urine and stool are coming out.

Newborns feed frequently around the clock and may poo during or just after a feed. Check often, learn what is normal for your baby and follow the plan from your midwife, neonatal team or health visitor. This matters particularly with prematurity, jaundice, sleepy feeding, faltering growth or concerns about milk transfer.

If your care team asks for wet- and dirty-nappy counts, record what you actually observe. **An unlogged nappy is unknown; it is not evidence that the nappy was dry.**

Seek advice if the pattern changes sharply or there are fewer wet nappies than expected for your baby's age and feeding stage. Our [fewer-wet-nappies guide](/blog/fewer-wet-nappies-than-usual-baby.html) explains how to compare the pattern without using one arbitrary number for every baby.

## Change before, during or after the feed?

There is no single correct order. Choose the point that protects skin, supports feeding and creates the least disruption.

### Change before the feed when…

- you already know there is poo or a leak
- the nappy is so full it will distract the baby
- changing after a full feed tends to trigger discomfort or posseting
- your baby needs a little help waking for an advised newborn feed

Then feed and return the baby to sleep without a second routine change unless they poo again.

### Change mid-feed when…

- a sleepy newborn needs gentle waking to finish an important feed
- your baby commonly poos as feeding begins
- changing first causes distress before they have taken the edge off their hunger

Offer a little milk or one breast, pause for the change, then finish the feed. This can turn the second half into the settling phase.

### Change after the feed when…

- your baby often poos while feeding
- the nappy was only mildly wet at the start
- a clean, dry nappy helps them settle comfortably

Keep movements slow. If your baby has reflux or becomes uncomfortable after feeds, use the position and handling recommended by your healthcare professional; do not add wedges or sleep positioners.

## A low-stimulation 3am change

Prepare the space before lifting the baby. Put a clean nappy, cotton wool or suitable wipes, a bag for the used nappy and spare clothes within reach. A changing mat on the floor avoids fall risk when everybody is tired.

Then:

1. Use enough light to see the skin and nappy contents clearly. Dim does not mean dark.
2. Keep your voice quiet and skip play, but still respond warmly if your baby seeks you.
3. Clean from front to back, including skin folds, without scrubbing.
4. Use plain water and cotton wool, or fragrance- and alcohol-free wipes. Avoid soap, bubble bath, antiseptic and talcum powder on the nappy area.
5. Pat the skin dry and apply any barrier or prescribed cream as directed.
6. Fit the clean nappy snugly, with room at the waist and no folded-in leg cuffs.
7. Place your baby on their back in their own clear, firm, flat sleep space.
8. Wash your hands.

Never leave a baby unattended on a changing table, bed or other raised surface—not even for a second. If you are so sleepy that you may drift off, put the baby in their safe sleep space and wake another adult if available. Never fall asleep with a baby on a sofa or armchair.

## Repeated leaks are a fit problem, not a sleep-training problem

If a nappy leaks at roughly the same point every night, note what actually happened before changing the whole sleep routine:

- Was it saturated, or did urine escape while the nappy was still fairly light?
- Did a leg cuff fold in?
- Is the waistband too loose or the absorbent area sitting in the wrong place?
- Did a large bedtime feed or unusually long first stretch change the timing?
- Is the nappy suitable for your baby's current weight and the manufacturer's overnight guidance?

A correctly fitted, more absorbent product may help, but “size up” is not a universal answer: a nappy that is too large can gap and leak. Follow the manufacturer's size and fit instructions. Do not put two nappies on unless a qualified professional has specifically advised it; doubling can distort the fit and trap heat.

Review three ordinary nights rather than treating one leak as a permanent pattern. Log **why** the change was needed—poo, leak, very full, sore skin or routine only. That distinction is more useful than a simple yes/no count.

## Nappy rash: when sleep should not win

Nappy rash often improves with frequent changes, gentle cleaning, drying and time without a nappy. The NHS recommends changing wet or dirty nappies as soon as possible, using extra-absorbent nappies and checking the fit.

Speak to a pharmacist or health visitor if you need help choosing a barrier cream. Contact a health visitor or GP if the rash is not improving, is worsening or spreading, or your baby has a high temperature or seems very uncomfortable. Blisters, weeping skin, a rash extending beyond the nappy area or a baby who seems unwell also deserve prompt advice.

For a baby under three months, a temperature of **38°C or higher** needs urgent advice from NHS 111 or a GP. Call **999** if a baby is blue or grey, floppy, difficult to wake, having a seizure or struggling to breathe.

Blood in the nappy or unusually pale/white poo needs prompt professional advice. Black stool is normal meconium in the first newborn days, but later black or tar-like stool should be assessed rather than entered into an app and watched at home.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I wake a sleeping baby for a wet nappy?

Not automatically in every situation. For a comfortable older baby with healthy skin and a mildly wet, absorbent nappy that is not leaking, you may be able to wait until the next natural wake. Wake and change for poo, leakage, significant fullness, discomfort, rash or an individual clinical monitoring plan.

### Does a newborn need a nappy change at every night feed?

Check at each feed and follow your midwife's or neonatal team's guidance. Newborn nappies are useful evidence of intake and hydration, and newborns often poo during feeds. That does not mean every lightly wet nappy must be changed twice in one feeding session; it means observation and accurate recording matter.

### Is it better to change before or after a night bottle?

Change before if there is poo, a leak or a very full nappy. If your baby often poos while feeding, pause mid-feed or change afterwards. Keep bottle feeding responsive and never prop the bottle while changing.

### What about a dream feed?

Do not wake a settled baby for a routine nappy change just because you are offering a dream feed. Check for poo, leakage, discomfort and fullness. If the nappy is only mildly wet and your older baby is comfortable, a change may wake them more than the feed. See the full [dream-feed guide](/blog/should-i-dream-feed-my-baby.html).

### Can I use the wetness indicator to decide?

Use it as a prompt to check, not a measure of hydration or comfort. It cannot detect poo reliably, quantify urine or inspect skin.

### My baby has fewer wet nappies than usual—should I wait until morning?

No. Feed responsively and seek advice if output has fallen sharply or your baby has a dry mouth, sunken soft spot, unusual sleepiness, poor feeding or seems unwell. A dry nappy for 12 hours is an urgent warning sign in NHS guidance; younger babies or a significant change may need help sooner.

## The bottom line

Night nappy care is not “change every feed” versus “never wake the baby”. Check, identify what is actually in the nappy and respond to the baby in front of you.

Poo, leaks, soreness, discomfort and clinical monitoring win over sleep. A mildly wet nappy on a comfortable older baby may be able to wait. When a change is needed, keep it calm, safe and brief—then record the reason so tomorrow's decision is based on a pattern, not 3am guesswork.
