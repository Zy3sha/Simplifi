Banana disappears. Pear is greeted like an old friend. Broccoli is squeezed once, dropped over the side and ignored. By the end of the week, every fruit has been welcomed and every green vegetable is still on the tray.

Should you stop offering fruit, mix vegetables into something sweet or keep serving broccoli untouched forever?

**Keep fruit. Offer vegetables alongside it in tiny, pressure-free amounts, and let less-sweet vegetables appear often enough to become familiar.** Mixing fruit and vegetables is fine as one preparation, but also offer the vegetable where your baby can see, smell and taste it separately. The goal is not to trick a mouthful in today; it is to build recognition over time.

The NHS says babies may need 10 or more attempts to accept a new food. A funny face, one spat-out spoonful or a week of rejected cauliflower does not prove a permanent dislike.

> **Fruit is not the enemy. Pressure is not the solution. Familiarity is the useful middle.**

This guide is for a baby who began complementary foods at around six months, is safely managing the offered texture and otherwise seems well. Follow individual advice if your baby was premature, has a feeding condition, poor growth, significant eczema, known allergy or a clinical feeding plan.

## What to do at the next meal

Keep the experiment smaller than your worry.

1. **Serve a familiar food.** A little fruit, porridge, yoghurt or another accepted food can keep the plate emotionally safe.
2. **Add one tiny vegetable opportunity.** One very soft broccoli floret or a teaspoon of fine pea mash is enough.
3. **Keep it recognisable.** Put the vegetable beside the familiar food, or reserve a little separately if the rest is mixed.
4. **Model it.** Eat the same vegetable without asking your baby to copy you.
5. **Let baby lead.** Touching, smelling, licking, holding and spitting out are information-gathering.
6. **End without persuasion.** Repeated head turns, a closed mouth or distress means finished.
7. **Offer again another day.** Change preparation only when it helps texture or participation—not to disguise the ingredient completely.

Do not withhold milk or fruit to make your baby hungry enough to surrender. Babies under 12 months still need their usual breast milk or first infant formula; early solids complement milk rather than replacing it abruptly.

## Is it a vegetable problem—or something else?

The same rejected floret can have different explanations.

| What you notice | What may be happening | What to try next |
|---|---|---|
| fruit and sweet potato are accepted, broccoli and spinach are not | less-sweet or bitter flavours are less familiar | repeat one less-sweet vegetable in a tiny amount without pressure |
| vegetable purée is accepted but florets are refused | the texture or self-feeding skill may be the barrier | bridge with a thicker mash, then a squashably soft graspable piece |
| florets are explored but spoonfuls are refused | baby may want more control | offer a preloaded spoon and one safe finger-food form |
| vegetables are accepted at lunch but refused near bedtime | tiredness, not flavour, may be driving refusal | offer the learning food at an earlier calm meal |
| everything except fruit is refused after a full milk feed | baby may simply have little appetite | keep usual milk feeds and try solids at a calmer point next time |
| vegetables were eaten before illness or teething | temporary discomfort may have narrowed appetite | use familiar manageable food and return to variety when baby is well |
| coughing, choking, wet breathing or pain repeats with certain textures | possible feeding or swallowing difficulty | stop experimenting alone and seek professional assessment |

Change one variable at a time. If you swap broccoli for courgette, move lunch, change the chair and hide everything in pear, you will not know what helped.

## Why fruit often wins first

Fruit is usually soft, fragrant and naturally sweet. Many babies find it immediately rewarding. Less-sweet vegetables can taste more bitter or unfamiliar, and a baby is still learning what food is, how it moves in the mouth and whether each new sensation is safe.

That does not make fruit unhealthy or vegetables medicine. Both sit in the fruit-and-vegetable food group, while the wider weaning pattern also needs starchy foods, iron-rich proteins and suitable dairy or alternatives.

The NHS specifically recommends offering vegetables that are not sweet—such as broccoli, cauliflower and spinach—from the start. It also says funny faces do not necessarily mean dislike; a baby may simply be meeting a new flavour or texture.

Think of acceptance as a ladder:

1. tolerates the food nearby
2. touches or moves it
3. smells, licks or mouths it
4. bites and spits it out
5. swallows a small amount
6. accepts it on some days
7. recognises it as an ordinary food

Only one rung involves an impressive mouthful. The earlier rungs still reduce novelty.

## Should I mix vegetables with fruit?

**Yes, sometimes. Do not make mixing the only way the vegetable ever appears.**

A pear-and-broccoli mash is still a real food combination. It can soften a strong first taste, add moisture and create a manageable texture. Family food is naturally mixed too: lentil sauce contains tomato; porridge may contain fruit; a curry may combine several vegetables.

The limitation is learning. If every green vegetable is hidden beneath much sweeter fruit, baby gets fewer clear chances to recognise the vegetable’s own flavour.

Use the **together-and-separate method**:

- serve a small mixed portion that feels manageable
- keep a teaspoon or one soft piece of the vegetable visible beside it
- name the food neutrally: “broccoli and pear”
- accept exploration of the separate piece without requiring it to be eaten
- gradually let the vegetable contribute more of the mixed flavour if baby remains comfortable

This preserves a successful meal while keeping the learning food honest.

## Mixing, pairing and hiding are different

| Approach | Example | Useful when | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| pairing | soft broccoli beside familiar pasta or pear | baby benefits from a safe food and a learning food | do not demand the vegetable before the familiar food |
| mixing openly | pea and potato mash, named as both | texture is easier together or the family dish is naturally mixed | keep occasional separate exposure so the flavour remains recognisable |
| gradual ratio change | mostly potato with a little spinach, then slightly more spinach | a strong flavour needs a gentle bridge | move at baby’s pace; no need to increase every meal |
| hiding | spinach secretly blended into a favourite with no visible or named exposure | may add a nutrient to that one dish | does little to teach recognition and can undermine trust if it changes a reliable food unexpectedly |

You do not need ingredient theatre. If vegetables are blended into a family sauce, serve the sauce normally and say what is in it. The goal is not a perfectly separated Montessori tray; it is a baby who meets food without deception or pressure.

## A seven-offer vegetable ladder

This is seven **opportunities**, not seven consecutive days and not seven required bites. Repeat the same vegetable across two or three weeks when baby is well.

### Offer 1: visible and tiny

Place a teaspoon or one safe soft piece beside a familiar food. Let baby ignore it.

### Offer 2: model without prompting

Eat the same vegetable. Avoid “Mmm! You love it!” or asking for a performance. Calm normality is enough.

### Offer 3: change control, not flavour

If spoon-fed, add a preloaded spoon or finger-food form. If self-feeding, offer a little mash for dipping or scooping.

### Offer 4: use an open mixture

Combine it with potato, lentils, yoghurt where suitable, or a little fruit, while keeping the vegetable named and a tiny separate sample available.

### Offer 5: change preparation

Roasted courgette may feel different from steamed courgette. Broccoli can be fine-mashed, thicker mash or a very soft whole floret with a graspable stem. Keep every form appropriate for baby’s current skills.

### Offer 6: move the timing

Try the vegetable at the calmest meal, before tiredness or distraction peaks. Do not create extreme hunger by delaying milk.

### Offer 7: repeat the easiest version

Familiarity grows through repetition, not constant escalation. If fine-mashed broccoli was comfortably touched, repeat that success before moving on.

At the end, ask whether baby is less wary—not whether the bowl is empty.

## Five low-waste vegetable combinations

Each keeps the vegetable identifiable and adds something nutritionally useful. Adapt texture to your baby.

### Broccoli + lentil

Very soft broccoli with fine-mashed red lentils gives a less-sweet vegetable beside an iron-rich pulse. Add tomato or another vitamin-C-containing food when convenient.

### Pea + potato

Potato softens the flavour and thickens pea mash without turning it into dessert. Offer a few squashed peas only when prepared safely for the baby’s stage; whole round firm foods can be a choking risk.

### Spinach + egg

Finely chopped cooked spinach can sit in well-cooked egg in a manageable form. Egg is a common allergen: introduce it in a small amount on its own timetable and follow individual allergy advice.

### Cauliflower + yoghurt

Soft cauliflower can be mashed with pasteurised full-fat plain yoghurt where dairy has already been introduced and tolerated. Keep some cauliflower visible.

### Courgette + bean

Soft courgette with mashed white beans creates an iron-containing, less-sweet option with a smooth-to-lumpy bridge.

Avoid adding salt, stock cubes high in salt, sugar or honey. Honey is not suitable before 12 months.

## Do not let fruit become the entire meal plan

Fruit can stay on the menu, but a fruit-only pattern misses other priorities.

Across the week, include:

- **iron-rich foods:** meat, fish, egg, beans, lentils, tofu and iron-fortified cereal
- **starchy foods:** potato, oats, rice, pasta, bread and other suitable grains
- **vegetables and fruit:** including less-sweet vegetables and varied colours
- **full-fat pasteurised dairy:** such as plain yoghurt or cheese where suitable
- **usual milk feeds:** breast milk or first infant formula during the first year
- **common allergens:** introduced safely, one at a time initially and then kept in the diet if tolerated

The goal is variety over time, not every food group arranged perfectly at each meal.

## What to track for ten vegetable offers

Do not count mouthfuls. Record enough to tell flavour refusal from timing or texture.

| Record | Why it helps |
|---|---|
| vegetable and preparation | distinguishes broccoli mash from a soft floret |
| separate, paired or mixed | shows whether recognition changes with presentation |
| time and nearby milk feed | reveals tired or full meal windows |
| response: ignored, touched, mouthed, spat, swallowed | captures progress before quantity |
| texture | separates flavour learning from oral-skill difficulty |
| familiar food present | shows whether a safe pairing made exploration easier |
| useful context | teething, illness, constipation, nursery or distraction can explain an off meal |

Review after ten offers, not after every lunch. Look for a softer response: less turning away, more touching, longer exploration or acceptance in one form.

## When vegetable refusal needs more support

A baby preferring fruit while still exploring a range of food groups is common. Speak to your health visitor, GP or paediatric dietitian when:

- the accepted range keeps narrowing rather than expanding
- baby refuses most foods or textures, not only vegetables
- meals are regularly distressing or painful
- coughing, choking, wet or gurgly breathing, colour change or recurrent vomiting happens with meals
- baby struggles with smooth foods or liquids as well as lumps
- wet nappies reduce, feeding changes or growth concerns you
- a previously managed eating skill is suddenly lost

A suspected allergic reaction is different from a funny face or refusal. Stop the food and seek appropriate medical advice. Call 999 for severe breathing difficulty, swelling affecting the airway, collapse, marked floppiness or another life-threatening reaction.

## Mealtime safety

- sit baby fully upright in a stable highchair with the harness secured
- stay within arm’s reach for the entire meal
- test that cooked vegetables are squashably soft
- adapt shape and texture to the baby’s current skill
- avoid hard raw vegetable chunks, whole nuts, popcorn and other choking hazards
- learn the difference between gagging and choking and take baby first-aid training
- do not put fingers blindly into a gagging baby’s mouth

No amount of vegetable variety compensates for an unsafe shape or unsupervised meal.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I stop giving fruit until my baby eats vegetables?

No. Fruit is nutritious and can remain part of a varied diet. Offer a tiny less-sweet vegetable beside familiar food and repeat it without pressure. Restricting accepted food to create hunger can make meals more anxious.

### Is it bad to mix broccoli with pear?

No. Name both foods and keep occasional separate broccoli exposure so the vegetable remains recognisable. Over time, adjust the ratio only if baby stays comfortable.

### How many times should I offer a rejected vegetable?

There is no pass mark, but the NHS says acceptance may take 10 or more attempts. Space offers across calm meals and count exploration. Stop and seek advice if refusal comes with pain, swallowing difficulty, illness or a possible reaction.

### What are less-sweet vegetables for babies?

The NHS names broccoli, cauliflower and spinach. Courgette, green beans and some leafy vegetables can add other less-sweet tastes when cooked and prepared safely for the baby’s stage.

### Does a funny face mean my baby hates broccoli?

No. A surprised expression can be a normal response to a new flavour or texture. Look for repeated turning away or distress and respect fullness cues, but do not turn one grimace into a permanent food preference.

### Should I hide vegetables in sauce?

Vegetables can naturally be part of sauces and mixed family meals. Tell baby what is being served and continue offering recognisable vegetables separately sometimes. Hidden intake alone does not build familiarity with the food’s own taste and appearance.

### What if my baby throws every vegetable?

Throwing can mean exploration, finished, tired or not interested. Keep portions tiny, offer only one or two pieces at a time and end when baby repeatedly signals they are done. Do not keep returning the same thrown food to create a battle.

### My baby used to eat vegetables and suddenly stopped. Why?

Teething, illness, constipation, tiredness, a texture change or growing independence can narrow appetite temporarily. Return to a manageable form and seek advice if the loss persists, involves more foods or comes with swallowing, growth, hydration or illness concerns.

## The bottom line

Your baby can love fruit and still learn vegetables. Keep the accepted fruit, make one less-sweet vegetable a tiny ordinary part of the meal, and repeat it in recognisable, safe forms without chasing a bite.

Mixing can be a bridge. Pairing can preserve comfort. Separate exposure teaches recognition. None of them needs pressure.
