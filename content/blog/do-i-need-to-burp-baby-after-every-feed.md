The feed is finished. Your baby looks peaceful, but you are still patting their back ten minutes later because surely sleep cannot begin until the burp arrives.

**The short answer:** offer a brief, gentle opportunity to burp, especially if your baby seems uncomfortable or swallowed air—but you do not need to chase a burp from a content baby after every feed. The NHS says there is no single rule for timing: some babies need winding during a bottle, some afterwards, and a couple of minutes is usually enough.

A burp is not proof that a feed “worked”. No burp is not proof that air is trapped. Watch the baby, not the clock.

## The one-minute decision

| What you see | A sensible next step |
|---|---|
| Baby is feeding calmly | continue responsively; do not interrupt solely because a set number of minutes passed |
| Baby pulls away, squirms, grimaces or seems uncomfortable | pause the feed and offer a gentle burp |
| Feed is finished and baby is settled | hold upright briefly if you wish; stop if no burp comes after a couple of minutes |
| Baby is crying, arching, drawing legs up or clenching fists | trapped wind is one possibility; try winding, then a gentle tummy massage or bicycle legs while baby is awake |
| Baby repeatedly struggles during feeds or brings up large amounts | ask a health visitor, feeding specialist or GP to review the whole feed |
| Baby has green, yellow or bloody vomit, projectile vomiting, breathing difficulty, unusual sleepiness or will not feed | seek urgent medical advice; call 999 for emergency signs |

None of the discomfort signs is specific to wind. Hunger, tiredness, a fast milk flow, attachment, reflux, illness, temperature and simply needing contact can look similar. Try one calm step, then reassess instead of deciding every post-feed cry must be gas.

## Why do babies need burping?

Babies can swallow air alongside milk. A pocket of air in the stomach may feel uncomfortable until it comes back up. Bottle flow, the baby's position, crying before the feed and repeatedly losing suction can all affect how much air is swallowed.

Breastfed babies may swallow less air when they are deeply and comfortably attached, so some rarely need help to burp. Others still do—particularly with a fast let-down, shallow attachment, frequent coming on and off, or a combination of breast and bottle feeds. Feeding method cannot tell you what this particular baby needs at this particular feed.

The useful question is not “breast or bottle?” It is:

> **Does my baby look comfortable enough to continue or finish?**

## When should I burp my baby?

### During a bottle feed

Pause if your baby becomes tense, turns away, gulps, coughs, leaks milk, flattens the teat or seems to need a break. Bring the bottle down or remove it gently, sit the baby upright and offer a burp. Resume only when they show that they want more.

Do not use winding as a way to persuade a baby to finish. Turning away, closing the mouth, slowing down and relaxing the hands may be fullness cues. The [NHS bottle-feeding guide](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/breastfeeding-and-bottle-feeding/bottle-feeding/advice/) advises following the baby's lead rather than worrying about an unfinished bottle.

### During a breastfeed

If your baby is swallowing rhythmically and looks relaxed, there is no need to detach them for a scheduled burp. Try a pause if they repeatedly pull off, splutter, arch or become unsettled.

Persistent clicking, painful feeds, damaged nipples, difficulty staying attached or concern about milk transfer deserve skilled feeding support. Burping may relieve one uncomfortable moment; it cannot correct positioning, attachment, tongue movement or milk flow by itself.

### Between breasts

The natural pause when changing sides is an easy moment to try. If no burp appears and the baby wants the second breast, continue. If they are content and do not want more, do not treat the missing burp as a reason to restart the feed.

### After the feed

Hold the baby upright with their head and neck supported, keep their body reasonably straight, and gently rub or pat the upper back. If nothing happens after a couple of minutes and the baby is calm, you can stop.

Some babies burp later while being carried. Some make a tiny sound you barely notice. Some do not burp at all. None requires a twenty-minute percussion session.

## Three gentle burping positions

Choose the position in which you can support the baby securely and stay comfortable yourself. Use a muslin if your baby often brings up a little milk.

### 1. Upright against your shoulder

- rest the baby's chin near your shoulder without covering their face
- support the head, neck and shoulder area with one hand
- keep the torso upright rather than folded
- rub upwards or pat the upper back gently with the other hand

This is often easiest after a feed, but it can be tiring after birth or surgery. Sit down rather than pacing if you feel unsteady.

### 2. Sitting on your lap

- sit the baby facing away from you
- place your palm against their chest
- support the chin and jaw with your fingers without pressing the throat
- lean the baby slightly forwards and gently rub or pat the back

Keep the airway visible. The support is under the jaw, not around the neck.

### 3. Across your lap while awake

- place the baby face down across your lap
- keep the head supported and slightly higher than the chest
- support the chin without pressure on the throat
- rub or pat the back gently

This is an awake, actively supervised winding position—not a sleep position. If the baby becomes drowsy, move them to their clear, flat sleep space and place them on their back.

## How hard should I pat?

Gentle is enough. A slow rub up the back, small circles or soft cupped-hand pats can all work. You do not need to thump, bounce forcefully, press the stomach or keep changing position rapidly.

The goal is to make space for air to rise, not to knock it out. If your baby becomes more distressed, stop, hold them calmly and reconsider whether they are hungry, tired, overstimulated, too hot or cold, need a nappy change, or seem unwell.

## What if my baby will not burp?

If they are comfortable, nothing needs fixing. Put the absent burp down and move on.

If they seem windy, the NHS suggests laying them on their back while awake, gently massaging the tummy and moving the legs in a bicycle motion. Stop if it causes discomfort. These are soothing options, not a way to diagnose gas.

Do not:

- repeatedly restart a finished feed to “push the wind up”
- give extra milk solely because no burp appeared
- use gripe water, herbal remedies, anti-colic drops or medicine without checking suitability and current professional advice
- place a baby to sleep on their side or front to release wind
- raise the cot mattress, use a wedge or add a sleep positioner

For every sleep, return the baby to a clear, firm, flat sleep space on their back. If you are holding them upright after a night feed, stay awake. A sofa or armchair becomes dangerous if the adult may doze.

## Reduce swallowed air during bottle feeds

Burping is only one part of the feed. A calmer, responsive bottle technique may reduce the need to recover from a rushed flow afterwards.

- hold your baby close in a semi-upright position with the head supported
- brush the teat against the lips and let the baby draw it in
- hold the bottle nearly horizontal, only slightly tipped, so milk does not pour too quickly
- allow pauses and lower the bottle when the baby needs a break
- check a teat that repeatedly collapses or seems blocked
- never prop a bottle or leave a baby feeding alone
- stop when the baby shows they have had enough

There is no single “anti-colic” bottle that is best for every baby. Before repeatedly buying new equipment or changing formula, ask a health visitor or feeding specialist to watch a complete feed.

## Is it wind, reflux or colic?

These labels overlap in everyday conversation, but they are not interchangeable.

**Wind** describes swallowed air that may cause temporary discomfort. A burp can help.

**Reflux** means milk comes back up during or after feeding. A little effortless posseting is common in babies who are otherwise content, healthy and gaining weight. Burping may help some babies but does not “close” the stomach or cure reflux.

**Colic** describes repeated, prolonged crying in a baby who otherwise seems healthy, often in the early months. Wind may be considered among several possibilities, but a difficult evening is not proof that the parent failed to burp properly.

If reflux appears to bother your baby, the [NHS recommends feeding-position advice, holding baby upright during and after feeds, and regular burping during feeds](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/reflux-in-babies/). It also says the baby should still sleep flat on their back and the cot should not be raised.

## When to get feeding or medical help

Ask a midwife, health visitor, breastfeeding specialist or GP for advice if:

- feeds are repeatedly painful or distressing for either of you
- your baby coughs, splutters, arches or pulls away through most feeds
- they repeatedly refuse feeds or take much less than usual
- vomiting is frequent or they appear in pain
- they are not gaining weight as expected
- you are cycling through bottles, teats, formula changes or remedies without improvement
- crying after feeds is persistent and you cannot work out why

Ask for an urgent GP appointment or call **NHS 111** if your baby has green or yellow vomit, blood in vomit or poo, projectile vomiting, a swollen or tender tummy, cannot keep fluids down, shows dehydration, refuses to feed, or will not stop crying and is very distressed.

Call **999** for a baby who is unresponsive or floppy, has a seizure, has blue, grey, pale or blotchy skin, is very difficult to wake, or has serious breathing difficulty. For a baby under 3 months, a temperature of **38°C or higher** needs urgent medical advice.

Trust the baby in front of you. A burp should never become the explanation that delays help for an unusual cry, poor feeding or an unwell child.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should I try to burp my baby?

The NHS says a couple of minutes should usually be enough. If your baby is comfortable and no burp comes, stop. If distress continues, reconsider other causes or ask a health visitor for advice.

### Do breastfed babies need burping?

Some do and some do not. A well-attached breastfed baby may swallow little air, but fast flow, coming on and off or shallow attachment can change that. Be guided by comfort rather than applying a rule to every breastfeed.

### Should I burp my baby if they fall asleep feeding?

You can hold them upright briefly if you can remain fully awake. Do not keep stimulating a peaceful baby indefinitely to produce a burp. Return them to their own clear, flat sleep space on their back; do not sleep while holding them on a sofa or chair.

### Is spit-up after a burp normal?

A small amount of milk coming up without distress is common. Seek advice if vomiting is frequent, forceful, painful, affects feeding or growth, or worries you. Green, yellow, bloody or projectile vomit needs urgent advice.

### Does a bigger burp mean the feed went better?

No. Burp volume does not measure milk intake, feeding effectiveness or comfort. Look at responsive feeding, swallowing, wet nappies, growth and how your baby behaves across feeds.

### Do I need to buy an anti-colic bottle?

Not automatically. The NHS says there is no evidence that one bottle or teat type is best for every baby. Start with responsive positioning and flow, then ask somebody skilled to watch a feed if problems persist.

## The bottom line

Offer the burp; do not demand it. Pause when your baby looks uncomfortable, support their head and neck, keep the body upright and use a gentle rub or pat. After a couple of minutes, a content baby is allowed to be finished.

If distress keeps returning, zoom out from the missing burp. Feeding technique, attachment, flow, reflux, illness and the baby's wider pattern matter more than winning a contest with one bubble of air.
