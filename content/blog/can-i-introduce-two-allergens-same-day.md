You made scrambled egg on toast, then noticed the bread contains wheat—two foods your baby has never had. Or peanut butter went into yoghurt before you remembered dairy was new too. Have you done something dangerous?

**For planned first introductions, offer one new allergenic food at a time and in a small amount.** That is current NHS advice because, if a reaction occurs, there is one clear food to discuss. If your baby accidentally ate two new allergens together and remains well, do not panic or test the mixture again. Record the exact foods, ingredients, time and response, watch your baby, and make future introductions separately. Seek medical advice for any suspected reaction and call 999 for severe symptoms.

“One at a time” is an identification tool, not a claim that two allergens together cause an allergy.

## The answer changes when a food is already established

| What is in the meal? | Is it a clear first introduction? | Better plan |
|---|---|---|
| New egg + familiar avocado | Yes: egg is the only new allergen | Offer a small, well-cooked amount while baby is well and you can observe |
| New peanut + familiar tolerated yoghurt | Yes: peanut is new; dairy is established | Use safely thinned smooth peanut butter in the familiar food |
| New egg + new peanut | No: a reaction would be harder to attribute | Put one aside and introduce it on another suitable day |
| Familiar egg + familiar peanut | This is a repeat meal, not two introductions | Keep tolerated foods in the usual varied diet |
| A mixed packaged food with several untried allergens | No, unless every allergen except one is already established | Read the full ingredient list and choose a simpler first exposure |

The important distinction is **new versus already tolerated**, not the number of allergenic ingredients printed on the plate.

## Why one new allergen at a time?

The NHS recommends introducing foods that can trigger allergic reactions one at a time from around six months, in small amounts, so a response is easier to spot. This does not mean the baby's immune system cannot encounter more than one food in a day. It means a clean introduction produces more useful information.

Imagine hives appear after egg, peanut and yoghurt were first served together. You now know the meal may matter, but not which ingredient. A clinician may need a more cautious history and plan; you should not re-create the meal at home to identify the culprit.

Separate introductions also reduce memory errors. Parents can tell a health professional:

> “Egg was offered at 10am, it was fully cooked, this was the approximate amount, and these symptoms began at 10:20.”

That is much more useful than “It was some kind of breakfast bowl.”

## How far apart should new allergens be?

The main NHS parent guidance says **one at a time** but does not prescribe one universal multi-day waiting interval for every baby and food. Allergy UK advises choosing a day when baby is well, introducing one new allergenic food that day and offering it earlier in the day when supervision is easier.

A practical low-risk rhythm is:

1. choose one new allergenic food
2. offer a small amount in a safe texture with familiar foods
3. observe and record the response
4. follow the guidance or individual plan you have been given before adding the next new allergen
5. keep tolerated allergens in the ordinary diet

Do not create such long gaps that tolerated foods disappear or all introductions are delayed for months. NHS guidance says peanut and hen's egg should not be unnecessarily delayed beyond 6 to 12 months, and tolerated allergenic foods should become part of the baby's usual diet.

If your baby has eczema, a diagnosed food allergy, or a family history of food allergy, eczema, asthma or hay fever, speak to your GP or health visitor before introduction. A specialist plan takes priority over a general calendar.

## What if my baby already ate two new allergens together?

### If there are no symptoms

Stay calm. Write down:

- the exact food and every ingredient
- which allergens were genuinely new
- preparation and approximate amount eaten
- time offered
- whether baby remained well

Keep the packet or photograph the ingredient label when relevant. Do not immediately serve a bigger amount of both foods to “make sure”. A symptom-free combined meal is reassuring, but it is not as easy to interpret as separate exposures and does not provide a medical guarantee.

For future meals, return to one new allergen at a time. If your baby has higher allergy risk or you are uncertain how to separate the foods safely, ask a health visitor, GP or paediatric dietitian.

### If there may have been a mild reaction

Stop offering the suspected food or mixture and seek medical advice. Record what happened and when. Do not reintroduce either new allergen at home simply to identify which one caused it unless an appropriately qualified professional has given you a plan.

Possible food-allergy symptoms can include an itchy raised rash, swelling, vomiting, coughing, wheezing, a runny or blocked nose, red itchy eyes or worsening eczema. Symptoms and timing vary, and ordinary contact irritation around the mouth can look confusing. A diary supports assessment; it cannot diagnose the cause.

### When to call 999

Call **999** for signs of a severe allergic reaction, including:

- swelling of the tongue or throat
- difficult or noisy breathing, wheeze or a persistent cough
- difficulty swallowing or a hoarse voice
- sudden pallor, floppiness, collapse or loss of consciousness

If your child has an adrenaline auto-injector and allergy action plan, follow that plan immediately and call 999.

## Hidden double introductions parents often miss

The obvious pair is egg and peanut. Mixed foods create less obvious combinations:

- porridge made with cows' milk plus new peanut butter
- egg served on wheat toast when both are new
- hummus containing new sesame alongside new wheat pitta
- fish pie containing new fish, milk and wheat
- yoghurt mixed with a new tree-nut butter
- shop-bought veggie bites containing wheat, milk or sesame

The solution is not to fear recipes. Use simple foods while introductions are new, then combine them freely in safe family meals once the relevant ingredients are established and tolerated.

Read the label each time: ingredients can vary between brands and recipes. “Dairy-free” does not automatically mean free from every other allergen, and automatic food recognition in an app cannot verify cross-contamination warnings.

## Safe forms still matter

Allergy pacing and choking safety are different checks. A perfectly spaced peanut introduction is still unsafe if offered as whole nuts or a sticky spoonful of nut butter.

- **Egg:** use an appropriately cooked form and a texture your baby can manage.
- **Peanut and tree nuts:** use finely ground nuts or smooth nut butter thinned into a familiar food; never whole nuts.
- **Sesame:** use smooth tahini thinned into a suitable familiar food rather than a thick spoonful.
- **Fish:** cook thoroughly and remove every bone.
- **Shellfish:** cook thoroughly; do not serve raw or lightly cooked.
- **Milk:** pasteurised full-fat yoghurt or milk in food can be used from around six months, but cows' milk should not be the main drink before 12 months.

Sit baby upright, supervise every bite and choose a time when they are well, alert and not desperately hungry. ## A one-week example without an allergen race

This is an organisational example for a baby already ready for solids, not a clinical schedule:

| Day | Meal idea | What the day tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | New well-cooked egg + familiar avocado | Egg is the only new allergen |
| Tuesday | Familiar vegetables and milk feeds as usual | Ordinary variety; no checklist pressure |
| Wednesday | Tolerated egg again in a safe form | Egg moves toward the normal food rotation |
| Thursday | Familiar lentil and vegetable meal | Iron-rich food without another new allergen |
| Friday | New safely thinned smooth peanut butter + familiar fruit purée | Peanut is the only new allergen |
| Weekend | Familiar family foods, including tolerated allergens | Repetition becomes ordinary eating |

The exact days may differ under your health professional's plan. The principle is one identifiable new allergen, then regular tolerated foods—not completing a badge collection as quickly as possible.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I give egg and peanut on the same day?

For first introductions, offer them separately so any reaction is easier to attribute. If both are already established and tolerated, they can appear together in an age-appropriate meal.

### Does an established allergen count as “new” each time?

No. Familiar tolerated yoghurt beside a first peanut introduction is different from offering milk and peanut for the first time together.

### My baby had two new allergens and was fine. Are both now cleared?

A symptom-free meal is reassuring but not medical proof, and the combined exposure is harder to interpret. Record it and make subsequent servings clear and separate according to current guidance or your baby's individual plan.

### Should I delay allergens because I am anxious?

Do not delay indefinitely without advice. Current NHS guidance supports introducing allergenic foods from around six months when baby is ready and keeping tolerated foods in the usual diet. Ask your GP or health visitor for a tailored plan if risk or anxiety is making introduction difficult.
