Your baby usually leaves 30ml in the bedtime bottle, then wakes two hours later. It is hard not to think: **if they would just finish it, would everybody get a longer stretch?**

**The short answer: not reliably.** The NHS is direct: a big feed does not mean a baby will go longer between feeds. A baby may genuinely want a fuller feed on some evenings, but persuading them past their fullness cues can cause discomfort or overfeeding without preventing the next wake.

Offer enough milk, pace the bottle and let your baby decide when they are done. Treat the amount taken as information—not a sleep target.

> **A bedtime bottle is a feed, not a tank you have to fill to morning.**

## Why the “one more ounce” idea feels so convincing

Hunger can wake a baby, especially a newborn or young infant. So the logic seems tidy:

1. more milk creates more fullness
2. more fullness lasts longer
3. a longer gap means more sleep

Babies are not fuel gauges. The timing of the next feed depends on age, growth, the rest of the day's intake and an individual appetite—not only the final bottle. Wakes can also come from normal sleep cycling, temperature, noise, discomfort, a developmental change, illness or needing reassurance.

The amount shown on the bottle can make measured milk feel more controllable than everything else at bedtime. That does not make it the right lever.

## The bedtime-bottle decision table

| What is happening | A responsive next step |
|---|---|
| baby is actively sucking, swallowing comfortably and still showing hunger cues | continue at their pace |
| sucking slows, baby relaxes or pauses | tilt the bottle down and allow a break |
| baby turns away, pushes the teat out, closes their mouth or repeatedly stops | finish the feed; do not chase the remaining millilitres |
| baby drains the bottle and still roots or searches after a calm pause | offer a little more responsively rather than imposing a fixed limit |
| milk spills, baby gulps, coughs, splutters or looks panicked | stop the flow, support them upright and review the teat/feeding technique |
| baby seems uncomfortable, arches, repeatedly vomits or refuses feeds | seek feeding or medical advice; do not solve it with more volume |
| baby has an individual neonatal, growth or feeding plan | follow that plan rather than a sleep experiment |

Fullness cues can be subtle. The NHS lists stopping sucking, turning the head away, pushing the bottle away, spilling milk and splaying fingers or toes among the signs that a baby may need a break or be finished.

One cue is not a mathematical stop button. A baby may pause to breathe or wind, then actively invite the teat again. Give them that pause. The important difference is between **offering** and **persuading**.

## What responsive bedtime bottle feeding looks like

The bedtime feed can stay calm without becoming passive.

1. **Start with hunger, not the clock alone.** Rooting, hands to mouth, wriggling and opening the mouth are earlier cues than crying.
2. **Hold your baby close and semi-upright.** Support their head and neck without pinning the head in place, so they can turn away.
3. **Invite the teat.** Brush it against the top lip and wait for the mouth to open.
4. **Keep the bottle almost horizontal.** Tip it only enough to keep milk in the teat, allowing pauses rather than a continuous fast flow.
5. **Watch the baby, not the scale.** Slower sucking, relaxed hands, dribbling, turning away and pushing out the teat matter more than a round number.
6. **Pause and reassess.** Tilt the bottle down or remove it gently. Wind if your baby normally needs it, then offer again only if hunger cues return.
7. **Stop when your baby stops.** Never force them to finish.

This applies whether the bottle contains formula or expressed breast milk. The container changes; your baby's right to control the feed does not.

## Will a fuller baby ever sleep longer?

Sometimes a substantial feed and a longer first sleep stretch happen together. That still does not prove the extra volume created the sleep.

The baby may have:

- been hungrier because daytime feeds were unusually light
- reached a developmental stage with a naturally longer first stretch
- had a calmer nap day or better-timed bedtime
- fed more efficiently because the flow and position suited them
- simply had one different night

Conversely, a larger evening intake may coincide with more posseting, discomfort or wakes for some babies. A correlation can identify a question worth testing, but it cannot diagnose reflux or prove cause.

The useful question is not “what maximum bottle can my baby take?” It is:

> **Across ordinary evenings when my baby chooses different amounts, what happens next—and are they comfortable?**

## Do not deliberately underfeed to create a comparison

A safe comparison observes normal appetite. It does not ration milk on “small bottle” nights or pressure the baby on “large bottle” nights.

For 10–14 reasonably ordinary evenings:

- offer the bedtime/evening feeds responsively
- record the amount actually taken, not the amount prepared
- note clear fullness or feeding-difficulty cues
- log the bedtime and genuine night wakes
- mark illness, vaccination, travel or another disrupted day
- continue feeding overnight when your baby shows hunger

Then compare the naturally lower- and higher-volume evenings. If the amounts barely vary, there is no useful A/B comparison—and no reason to manufacture one.

One wake after one large bottle proves almost nothing. Teething, a short nap, a noisy neighbour or chance can dominate a single night.

## What if the next wake really is hunger?

A baby who wakes, roots, actively feeds and takes a substantial feed may genuinely be hungry. Night feeds are normal for young babies, and the NHS says babies are likely to need them for at least the first few months.

The safe response is not to force more milk into the previous feed. Instead, look at the whole 24 hours:

- Is feeding offered responsively during the day?
- Does the baby become distracted and take tiny daytime feeds?
- Are bottles paced with a suitable flow?
- Are wet and dirty nappies reassuring for age and stage?
- Is professionally measured growth following the baby's pattern?
- Is the baby feeding comfortably, without repeated coughing, pain or vomiting?

If an older baby takes most of their milk overnight and only snacks by day, use the [reverse-cycling guide](/blog/baby-feeds-more-at-night-than-day-reverse-cycling.html). If you are considering removing a night feed, use the [night-weaning readiness checklist](/blog/is-my-baby-ready-to-night-wean.html) and check with a health professional when growth or intake is uncertain.

Do not withhold a needed night feed to protect an experiment or a sleep score.

## What if my baby finishes every bottle?

An empty bottle does not automatically mean the amount was perfect—or insufficient. Fast flow can make it difficult for a baby to pause before the milk is gone.

Check the feed itself:

- Can your baby pause without milk continuing to pour?
- Is the bottle close to horizontal rather than tipped steeply?
- Are sucking, swallowing and breathing coordinated and comfortable?
- Does your baby have time to register fullness?
- Do they remain relaxed after the bottle, or seem uncomfortable and bring up a lot of milk?

After a pause, offer more if your baby still shows clear hunger. Do not cap a hungry baby's intake because an app has found a population average, and do not add milk merely because a chart says the bottle “should” be bigger.

Bottle sizes on formula packaging are preparation examples, not instructions to make a baby finish that amount.

## Reflux and the big bedtime bottle

Small amounts of effortless spit-up are common. Reflux becomes a clinical concern when feeding is painful or difficult, vomiting is frequent or forceful, growth is affected, or the baby seems unwell.

For reflux, the NHS recommends getting advice about feeding position, keeping the baby upright during and for as long as possible after feeds, and winding regularly. A healthcare professional may recommend smaller, more frequent feeds for some babies.

Keep safer sleep unchanged:

- put your baby on their back
- use a clear, firm, flat, separate sleep space
- do not raise the head of the cot
- do not use wedges or positioners
- do not leave a bottle in the cot

An upright post-feed hold requires an awake adult. If you might fall asleep, place your baby in their safe sleep space. Never feed or sleep with a baby on a sofa or armchair.

Seek urgent medical advice for green or yellow vomit, blood in vomit or poo, projectile vomiting, a swollen or tender tummy, inability to keep fluids down, feeding refusal, dehydration, unusual drowsiness or a baby who is very distressed. Call **999** for breathing difficulty, blue or grey colour, floppiness or a baby who will not wake normally.

## Never change the recipe to make a “heavier” bottle

More powder is not a sleep hack. Prepare formula with the exact water-to-powder ratio in the manufacturer's instructions, adding the correct water first and using the scoop supplied.

Do not:

- add an extra scoop of formula
- make a bottle with less water
- put cereal, baby rice, rusks, sugar or chocolate powder in it
- enlarge or cut the teat hole
- use a thickener or specialist formula without professional guidance

Incorrectly concentrated formula can make a baby unwell and contribute to dehydration or constipation. Adding cereal also creates feeding and choking concerns and does not turn a bottle into a safe sleep treatment.

First infant formula is all a formula-fed baby generally needs as their main milk through the first year. “Hungry baby”, comfort, anti-reflux and allergy products should not be chosen as DIY sleep solutions; discuss feeding concerns with a midwife, health visitor, pharmacist or GP.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I wake my baby for a top-up before I go to bed?

Not simply to force more calories in. A planned late feed is optional and does not reliably improve sleep. If you want to test a responsive dream feed for an older healthy baby, use the [dream-feed guide](/blog/should-i-dream-feed-my-baby.html) and stop if it adds disruption without improving parent sleep.

### My baby leaves 30ml, then wakes hungry. Should I make them finish?

No. Respect the stop cue and feed again when hunger returns. A baby's appetite can vary from feed to feed. If this pattern is persistent, review the whole day's feeding, flow, comfort, nappies and growth with a health professional rather than pushing the final 30ml.

### Is a bigger bottle different for expressed breast milk?

The same responsive principles apply. A bottle can continue flowing whether it contains formula or expressed milk, so pace it and stop at fullness cues. Pumped volume also does not measure your supply or what your baby transfers at the breast.

### Can I give a “hungry baby” formula for better sleep?

Do not switch formula to treat sleep without professional advice. First infant formula is generally suitable throughout the first year. Ask a health visitor, midwife, pharmacist or GP about feeding concerns or specialist products.

### When is bedtime-bottle refusal concerning?

Seek advice if refusal is persistent or comes with coughing, choking, painful feeds, repeated or forceful vomiting, fewer wet nappies, poor growth, unusual sleepiness or your baby seeming unwell. A sudden change matters more than one small bottle.

### Does OBubba tell me the correct bedtime bottle amount?

No. It records the amount actually taken and can compare naturally different evenings with the nights that followed. It does not prescribe a target bottle, estimate breast transfer or override hunger and fullness cues.

## The bottom line

A bigger bedtime bottle is not a reliable ticket to a longer night. Offer milk responsively, pace the feed, let your baby pause and stop when they say they are done.

If bedtime volume naturally varies, compare enough ordinary nights to see whether it matters for your baby. The answer may be no pattern at all—and that is useful. It frees you to stop chasing the last millilitres and look at the whole feeding day, comfort and sleep rhythm instead.
