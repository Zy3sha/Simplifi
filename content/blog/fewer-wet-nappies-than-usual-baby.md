The last two nappies felt lighter. Or perhaps your baby usually needs changing all day and suddenly the changing mat has been quiet.

**Fewer wet nappies than usual can be an early sign that a baby is taking in or retaining less fluid. It is not a diagnosis by itself.** The baby's age, feeding, urine colour, alertness, vomiting or diarrhoea, temperature and the direction of change all matter.

If your baby is unusually sleepy, difficult to wake, breathing quickly, has a dry mouth, few tears, sunken eyes or a sunken soft spot, or has markedly fewer wet nappies, get medical advice promptly. Call **999** for severe breathing difficulty, blue, grey, pale or blotchy colour, cold skin with worsening illness, collapse, or a baby who is very difficult to wake.

## What to do now

1. **Check the whole baby.** Look at colour, breathing, responsiveness, mouth, tears and whether the eyes or soft spot look sunken.
2. **Offer the usual milk responsively.** Continue breastfeeding or full-strength first infant formula. If your baby is being sick, smaller offers more often may be easier.
3. **Do not dilute formula.** Extra powder is unsafe too: make every feed exactly as the manufacturer instructs.
4. **Record the time and what the nappy felt like.** A change from heavy and pale to light, infrequent or dark matters more than an immaculate spreadsheet.
5. **Look for fluid losses.** Vomiting, watery diarrhoea, fever and hot weather can change the risk quickly.
6. **Ask for help if the direction is worsening.** For a child under five, NHS guidance directs parents to NHS 111 when dehydration signs are present.

Do not wait for a target count if your instincts say your baby is unwell. A young baby can deteriorate quickly, and one very concerning symptom matters more than an app trend.

## How many wet nappies should a newborn have?

Output normally rises over the first days as milk intake increases. NHS and NHS-affiliated feeding guidance uses an age-by-day pattern rather than expecting a day-one newborn to produce six heavy nappies immediately.

| Baby's age | A practical wet-nappy guide over 24 hours | What else to notice |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | at least 1 | urine may be small in volume; feeding support should already be available |
| Day 2 | at least 2 | nappies should begin becoming more frequent |
| Day 3 | at least 3 | urine should be moving towards paler; stools should be changing |
| Day 4 | at least 4 | nappies should feel progressively heavier |
| Day 5 | at least 5 heavy wet nappies | milk transfer, swallowing and yellow stools also help assess feeding |
| Day 6 onwards | around 6 heavy wet nappies | clear or pale-yellow urine is reassuring |

These are feeding-assessment guides, not a reason to wait until tomorrow. Contact your midwife or feeding team if nappies are not increasing as expected, feeding is painful or ineffective, stools are not changing, your baby is very sleepy or you are worried.

Disposable nappies can hide how much urine they contain. The [NHS suggests adding 2 to 4 tablespoons of water to a clean unused nappy](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/breastfeeding-and-bottle-feeding/breastfeeding-problems/enough-milk/) so you can feel what a wet nappy is like. Do not rely on a thin colour-changing line alone.

## What about an older baby?

After the early newborn period, a single universal number becomes less useful. Nappy absorbency, night sleep, milk and solids intake, hot weather and the baby's own rhythm can change when nappies appear.

Ask three questions:

- **Is this clearly fewer or lighter than my baby's usual pattern?**
- **Has the change lasted across more than one part of the day?**
- **Is anything else different—feeding, alertness, tears, mouth, urine colour, vomiting, diarrhoea or fever?**

A long overnight stretch can produce a different schedule without dehydration if the baby wakes well, feeds normally, has pale urine and returns to their usual output. A dry overnight nappy alongside poor feeding, dark urine or unusual drowsiness is a different picture.

## Signs that need advice now

The [NHS lists fewer wet nappies than usual](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/dehydration/) among dehydration signs in babies. Other signs include:

- a dry mouth, lips or tongue
- few or no tears when crying
- sunken eyes
- a sunken fontanelle, the soft spot on the head
- dark yellow, strong-smelling urine
- drowsiness or unusual irritability
- fast breathing or a fast heart rate

Call your GP or **NHS 111** promptly if these signs appear, your child stops breast or bottle feeding while ill, cannot keep fluids down, or you are worried about a baby under 12 months. Babies under three months, premature babies and babies with a feeding, kidney, heart or growth plan need an especially low threshold for advice.

Call **999** or go to A&E for signs of shock or serious illness, including severe breathing difficulty, blue or grey colour, marked pallor or blotching, cold skin, collapse, confusion, or being much sleepier than normal or difficult to wake.

## Feeding when nappies have dipped

Continue to offer breastfeeds. For formula-fed babies, use the usual formula at its normal strength and try smaller amounts more often if nausea makes a full bottle difficult. Never make formula weaker in an attempt to add water.

The [NHS dehydration guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/dehydration/) says a pharmacist can recommend an oral rehydration solution when appropriate. It is designed to replace water and salts in the right balance. Do not improvise a homemade salt-and-sugar drink for a baby, and do not use fruit juice or fizzy drinks; they can make diarrhoea or vomiting worse.

For a baby under six months, do not substitute bottles of plain water for missed milk unless a healthcare professional tells you to. Formula-fed babies may sometimes be offered small sips of cooled boiled water in hot weather **as well as** their usual feeds. From around six months, water can be offered in a cup with meals, while breast milk or first infant formula remains the main drink through the first year.

If vomiting or diarrhoea is present, ask a pharmacist, GP or NHS 111 how oral rehydration solution fits your baby's age and symptoms. A blog cannot assess whether home rehydration is enough.

## Wet nappies do not measure milk supply by themselves

Wet nappies are a useful piece of feeding evidence, but they do not measure breast-milk volume or diagnose low supply. Combine them with:

- rhythmic sucking and audible swallowing
- a comfortable, effective latch
- the baby's behaviour before and after feeds
- age-appropriate stools in the early weeks
- weight gain assessed on the growth chart
- a feeding observation by a midwife, health visitor or breastfeeding specialist when needed

For formula feeding, the number written on the bottle is not the only truth either. A baby may leave different amounts at different feeds. Responsive feeding, growth, urine output and clinical wellbeing belong in the same picture.

## A useful handover sentence

When calling for advice, numbers are most helpful when paired with the baby's baseline and behaviour:

> “She usually has about seven clearly wet nappies in 24 hours. She has had four today and five yesterday. The last urine looked darker, she has taken less milk, and she is sleepier than usual. Her last wet nappy was at 2:30pm.”

Also mention:

- the baby's exact age and whether they were premature
- breast, formula or mixed feeding and what changed
- the time of the last effective feed
- vomiting, diarrhoea, fever or hot-weather exposure
- urine colour and whether nappies felt normally heavy
- medicines and any medical or feeding plan

This is more actionable than “the app says dehydration”. The app should organise observations, never replace them.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is one dry nappy an emergency?

Not automatically. Check the baby's age, time since the last properly wet nappy, feeding, urine colour, mouth, tears, alertness and any illness. Seek prompt advice if output is markedly below normal or other dehydration signs appear. Do not wait for a second dry nappy if the baby seems unwell.

### Can a baby have fewer wet nappies after sleeping longer?

Timing can shift when a baby sleeps a longer stretch, but total output and the whole baby still matter. A well baby who wakes alert, feeds normally and returns to pale, normally wet nappies is different from a baby with poor feeding and persistent low output.

### Does a wet-nappy dip prove low breast-milk supply?

No. It is one reason to review effective feeding and milk transfer. Ask a midwife, health visitor or breastfeeding specialist to observe a feed if nappies, weight gain, swallowing or comfort concern you.

### Should I give extra water?

Do not replace milk with plain water. Fully breastfed babies do not normally need water before solids. Formula-fed babies under six months may need small sips of cooled boiled water in hot weather as well as usual feeds. For illness or possible dehydration, ask about oral rehydration solution rather than improvising.

### Can OBubba diagnose dehydration?

No. It can surface a reliably logged nappy trend and a separate low-output safety prompt. It cannot examine the baby or decide whether home care is safe. Use NHS 111, your GP or emergency services according to the baby's signs.
