Perhaps your midwife mentioned a questionnaire. Perhaps an app asked whether you have been able to laugh, cope or look forward to things. Perhaps you saw the phrase **Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale** and wondered whether the result would put a diagnosis—or a judgement—next to your name.

The **EPDS is a ten-question screening tool about how the past seven days have felt**. It looks for a pattern of low mood, loss of enjoyment, self-blame, anxiety, panic, feeling unable to cope, sleep difficulty caused by unhappiness, sadness, crying and thoughts of self-harm.

It does not ask whether you love your baby. It does not measure whether you are a good parent. It does not diagnose postnatal depression by itself.

> **Think of the EPDS as a door into a conversation, not a verdict at the end of one.**

## What does EPDS stand for?

EPDS stands for **Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale**. It was designed to help identify people who may need a fuller mental-health assessment during the perinatal period.

The questionnaire has ten items. For each one, you choose from four answers describing how often or how strongly that experience applied during the previous seven days. Each response scores from 0 to 3, producing a total between 0 and 30.

NICE includes the EPDS among validated self-report questionnaires that can be used as part of a full assessment. NHS England's 2026 Maternal Care Bundle describes it as a follow-up assessment after initial emotional-wellbeing questions or whenever there is concern.

The words **screening tool** matter. A high result is not a diagnosis. A low result does not cancel something you know is wrong.

## What do the ten questions cover?

Without turning the questionnaire into an exam to revise for, the items explore five broad areas:

| Area | What the questions are trying to notice | What they are not judging |
|---|---|---|
| enjoyment | whether laughter and looking forward to things feel different | whether you appear cheerful to other people |
| anxiety | worry, fear or panic that feels difficult to explain or control | whether you worry about your baby at all |
| coping | self-blame and the sense that ordinary demands are getting on top of you | whether the washing is done or the house is tidy |
| low mood | sadness, crying and sleep difficulty linked to unhappiness | ordinary waking because your baby needs you |
| safety | whether thoughts of self-harm have occurred | whether you are “strong enough” to parent |

There is no question about being perfectly calm, grateful or bonded every minute. New parenthood contains mixed feelings. The scale is looking for a pattern that deserves attention.

## How should I answer it?

Answer for **the past seven days**, not your hardest day since birth and not the version of yourself you hope to be tomorrow.

### Choose what is closest, not what is perfect

The answer may not describe you exactly. Pick the closest option. Do not average your way towards the answer that seems least worrying.

### Include the private moments

Answer from how you actually felt, including the hours when nobody else could see. Looking capable at a baby group or replying “fine” to messages does not make private distress less real.

### Separate baby waking from unhappy sleeplessness

One item asks about difficulty sleeping because you have felt unhappy. That is different from being awake solely because your baby woke. If you cannot sleep even when somebody else has the baby, or thoughts race when there is a genuine chance to rest, that distinction is useful to mention.

### Do not answer for somebody else's comfort

The questionnaire is not a pass–fail test and an honest answer is not a confession of bad parenting. Its purpose is to make support easier to offer.

### Ask for help if language or accessibility gets in the way

You should be able to complete a health assessment in a language and format you understand. Ask for an interpreter, accessible version or help reading the questions if needed. The professional reviewing it should still give you privacy to answer for yourself.

## What does an EPDS score mean?

Each item scores 0 to 3, so the total ranges from 0 to 30. A higher total suggests more symptoms were reported during that week. It does not measure love, competence or the certainty of a diagnosis.

Thresholds can vary between services, countries, research settings and the purpose of the screen. Current NHS England maternal-care guidance describes a positive screen as:

- a total score of **13 or above**
- a score of **2 or above on question 10**, which relates to self-harm
- **continued clinical concern**, regardless of the numbers

A positive screen should lead to a compassionate conversation, review of needs and agreed next steps—not an automated label.

Some services use lower thresholds to open an earlier conversation. That is one reason not to interpret a score without the guidance shown by the service or app that administered it.

## Why the total is not the whole story

Two people can reach the same total through very different experiences. One may mainly report anxiety and panic; another may report loss of enjoyment and sadness. Their history, safety, support, sleep, physical recovery and previous mental health all affect what help is appropriate.

The safety question also needs its own attention. A total can be low while the response to that question is concerning.

If you have thoughts of harming yourself, tell a GP, midwife, health visitor or mental-health professional today. If the thoughts feel like something you may act on, you have a plan, or you cannot keep yourself or your baby safe, call **999** or go to **A&E now**. In England, you can call **NHS 111 and select the mental-health option** for urgent support when there is not an immediate danger to life.

Do not wait to calculate the “right” score before asking for help.

## What if my score is low but I still feel wrong?

Say so. A questionnaire can miss:

- symptoms that started after the seven-day window
- distress you minimised or found hard to name
- intrusive thoughts, trauma symptoms, obsessive checking or severe anxiety
- a sudden change in behaviour or thinking
- physical illness affecting mood and energy
- the effect of domestic abuse, isolation, money, housing or feeding pressure
- how unsafe or unable to cope you feel in this moment

NHS England explicitly includes continued clinical concern as a reason for a compassionate conversation even when a numeric threshold is not reached.

You can say:

> **“My score may be below the cut-off, but I do not feel like myself and I am struggling to cope. I need somebody to look at the whole picture.”**

A lower result is a snapshot, not a promise that the next week will feel the same.

## What if my score is high?

A high result means it is worth sharing the answers with a healthcare professional. It does not mean you have failed, that treatment will automatically involve medication, or that your baby will be taken away.

The next conversation may cover:

- which symptoms feel hardest
- whether you feel safe
- how long this has been happening
- previous depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder, psychosis or trauma
- practical support and sleep opportunity
- physical recovery, pain, thyroid or anaemia concerns
- talking therapies, peer support and specialist perinatal services
- a safety and follow-up plan

Treatment can help. The NHS advises speaking to a GP, midwife or health visitor if you think you may have postnatal depression, even if you have only some signs.

## EPDS is not a postpartum-psychosis screen

Postpartum psychosis is a serious illness that often begins suddenly after birth and can worsen quickly. An EPDS total should never delay urgent action for:

- hallucinations—hearing, seeing, smelling or feeling things that are not there
- fixed unusual beliefs or intense suspiciousness
- severe confusion or behaviour that is very unlike you
- feeling extremely high, overactive or unable to slow thoughts and speech
- rapidly changing moods
- being unable or feeling no need to sleep despite having the opportunity

The NHS says postpartum psychosis is a medical emergency. Request an urgent same-day assessment from a GP; call NHS 111 if you cannot reach one or do not know what to do. Call 999 or go to A&E if there is imminent danger.

## Before you start: make a two-minute support plan

A screening result can feel exposing. Prepare one small next step before tapping the first answer:

1. Choose a time when you can answer privately.
2. Save the number for your GP, midwife or health visiting team.
3. Tell one trusted person you are doing a check-in, if that feels safe.
4. Decide where the baby can be safely placed if you become overwhelmed.
5. Remember that you can stop and seek human help at any point.

If another person is pressuring you to change an answer, complete the screen privately and tell a health professional when it is safe to do so.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the EPDS a diagnosis of postnatal depression?

No. It is a screening tool used to identify symptoms and support a fuller assessment. Diagnosis and care planning require a conversation with a healthcare professional.

### What is a “normal” EPDS score?

There is no score that proves somebody is coping or safe. NHS England currently uses 13+ as one positive-screen threshold, but the safety question and professional concern are considered separately. Services may use thresholds differently.

### Can I take the EPDS before birth?

The tool is used during pregnancy as well as after birth. NHS England's maternal-care guidance includes perinatal screening at antenatal and postnatal appointments.

### Can poor sleep raise the score?

The questions ask about the previous week, so a very difficult week may affect answers. One sleep item specifically asks about difficulty sleeping linked to unhappiness, not simply waking to care for a baby. A clinician should interpret the result in context.

### Should I repeat it?

Repeating a recognised screen can help show change when a healthcare professional or service recommends it. Do not use daily retesting to chase a reassuring number. If you feel worse or unsafe, ask for help rather than waiting for another score.

### Does OBubba share my answers with my baby's carers?

The current Flutter implementation does not put individual EPDS answers into the baby's shared care timeline. It retains a local completion time and total-score history. The app cannot send a result to your clinician, so share it yourself if you want it reviewed.
