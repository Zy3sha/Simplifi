The baby is finally asleep. The sound machine is humming. Now the timer asks the question nobody wanted at 9:47pm: **15 minutes, one hour or off?**

**The short answer:** there is no evidence that every baby needs white noise running until morning. The safer default is to place the sound source far away, use the lowest effective level and limit the duration—often with a timer. If turning it off reliably wakes your baby, do not respond by making it louder. Test a gentler fade, a longer but still bounded timer, or a quieter room setup and compare several nights.

The American Academy of Pediatrics advises families who use infant sleep machines to put them as far from the baby's head as possible, set them as low as possible and limit how long they play. White noise is an optional masking tool, not a treatment, a safer-sleep intervention or something a healthy baby must learn to tolerate.

> **Start with the least sound that solves a real sound problem.**

## The two questions hiding inside “all night”

Parents are usually trying to solve one of two different problems.

### “Will the boiler, traffic or another child wake the baby?”

A steady background may make sudden noises less noticeable by reducing the contrast between quiet and a bang, bark or floorboard. In that case, sound has a specific job.

Ask when the disruption actually happens. If the noisy dishwasher finishes at 10pm, an eight-hour soundscape may be more than the problem requires. If traffic is intermittent all night, first consider quieter room changes—closing the window safely, moving the cot away from the shared wall or placing soft furnishings outside the cot—before using continuous sound to overpower it.

### “Will the baby wake because the sound stops?”

They might. A sudden change in any sleep condition can be noticeable, just like a door clicking shut or a parent leaving. That does not prove the brain needs white noise for healthy sleep. It means the change itself may have become a cue.

Try changing one variable at a time:

- lower the sound slightly before shortening it
- move from all night to 60 minutes, not immediately to five
- keep the same bedtime phrase, dark room and sleep space
- test for three to five comparable nights rather than judging one wake
- return to the previous step during illness, travel or an unusually noisy week

You do not need to “wean” a baby from sound if it is quiet, distant and working for your family. You also do not have to preserve it forever because one timer experiment went badly.

## A practical timer decision table

| What is happening | A useful first test |
|---|---|
| room is already quiet | try no added sound; ordinary household noise does not need to be erased |
| settling is disrupted by evening bustle | use a low sound during the wind-down and a 15–30 minute timer |
| baby wakes as a 30-minute timer ends | try a quieter 45–60 minute period or lower the volume gradually before shortening |
| one predictable noise happens later | time the sound around that period rather than defaulting to dawn |
| environmental noise continues all night | reduce the source where possible; if masking is still useful, keep it distant and at the lowest effective level and discuss prolonged use with a health visitor or audiology professional if worried |
| baby only sleeps when the sound is loud | do not keep increasing it; move the device farther away, turn it down and review the wider sleep setup |
| baby is crying unusually, hard to wake or struggling to breathe | stop troubleshooting sleep and seek medical help |

A timer is not a moral verdict. It is a small experiment that prevents temporary sound from becoming an automatic eight-hour exposure without anybody deciding.

## What does the 2026 pink-noise study change?

A 2026 laboratory study in the journal *SLEEP* has understandably generated alarming headlines. Researchers studied **25 healthy adults**, not babies. Across seven monitored nights, continuous pink noise at 40 or 50 dBA reduced REM sleep in a dose-dependent way. When pink noise was added to intermittent environmental noise, its masking effect did not create a net improvement in sleep architecture.

That is important evidence against assuming “pink” automatically means restorative. It is **not** evidence that one night of low white noise harms a baby, and it cannot tell parents the exact effect of each OBubba sound on infant sleep. Adult sleep, infant sleep and different sound spectra are not interchangeable.

The honest conclusion is more modest:

- there is no proven best colour of noise for baby sleep
- a softer-sounding colour is not a safety rating
- evidence for long-term continuous broadband noise in children remains limited
- more sound and longer exposure should not be treated as automatically better

A 2024 scoping review likewise concluded that optimal intensity and duration in children still need more research. It supports limiting maximum output and duration rather than promising that a particular colour improves development.

## White, pink or brown: does the timer advice change?

No. The names describe the shape of the sound, not how safe it is.

- **White noise** spreads energy evenly by frequency and often sounds like a bright hiss.
- **Pink noise** reduces higher-frequency energy and often sounds softer or more balanced.
- **Brown noise** emphasises the lower end more strongly and sounds deep or rumbly.
- **Rain, ocean, fan, womb and shush sounds** are different textures, but they still create sound exposure.

Pick the least irritating steady sound at the lowest useful level. Avoid tracks with thunder, adverts, spoken surprises, dramatic volume changes or audible clicks in the loop. Listen to the whole track yourself before using it for sleep.

Do not infer that brown noise can run louder because it feels less sharp to an adult. Distance, output and duration still matter, and low frequencies can travel through walls and furniture differently.

## How low is “low”?

A percentage on a phone or speaker is not a decibel measurement at the cot. Thirty per cent on one device can produce a different sound level from thirty per cent on another. The sound file, speaker, room, case, Bluetooth connection and distance all change what reaches the baby.

One 2014 study measured 14 infant sleep machines at maximum volume. All exceeded 50 dBA at 30 cm, and three exceeded 85 dBA. The **50 dBA figure was a hospital-nursery reference used by the researchers**, not a universal guarantee that every home setup below it is risk-free.

Use the practical controls you do have:

1. put the device as far from the baby's head as practical
2. keep it off the cot, cot rail and nearby shelf
3. start at the lowest setting
4. listen from where the baby's head rests
5. shorten the exposure with a timer
6. recheck after changing the phone, speaker, sound or room

A consumer sound-meter app can flag an obviously loud setup, but an uncalibrated phone is not a medical-grade meter. If you are concerned about hearing, sound sensitivity or prolonged exposure, ask an audiology professional or your health visitor.

## Keep sound outside the safe-sleep equation

White noise does not prevent sudden infant death syndrome and cannot make an unsafe surface safer. For every sleep:

- place your baby on their back
- use a clear, firm, flat, separate sleep space
- keep pillows, bumpers, toys, nests and loose bedding out
- keep the phone, speaker and every cable completely outside the cot and out of reach
- make sure you can still hear smoke alarms, carbon-monoxide alarms, monitor alerts and your baby's cry

Never put earbuds, headphones or earplugs on a baby. Although earplugs helped the adults in the 2026 laboratory study, that finding is **not** a baby-sleep recommendation; small objects create serious risks and babies need caregivers to hear and respond to them.

If your baby is blue or grey, floppy, very difficult to wake, having a seizure or struggling to breathe, call **999**. White noise should never mask a symptom or delay a safety check.

## A seven-night “do we need it?” experiment

Do not turn bedtime into a research project. A tiny comparison is enough.

### Nights 1–2: observe the current setup

Write down:

- which sound plays
- where the device sits
- the approximate slider position
- whether it runs all night or on a timer
- the environmental noise you are trying to mask
- when the first wake happens

You are not trying to prove that sound caused every wake. You are creating a baseline.

### Nights 3–5: change one thing

Choose only one:

- move the device farther away
- lower the volume one small step
- change from all night to 60 minutes
- keep the timer but remove a sudden end by lowering the level first

Keep the rest of the bedtime routine broadly similar. Comfort and feed your baby responsively; do not withhold support to protect an experiment.

### Nights 6–7: keep, adjust or stop

If sleep looks essentially the same, keep the quieter or shorter setup. If wakes repeatedly line up with the sound stopping, extend the timer modestly or taper more gradually. If sleep worsens for reasons that have nothing to do with noise—teething, illness, hunger, nap changes—pause the comparison.

The result can be “we still prefer low sound overnight”. The useful part is that the choice became intentional rather than automatic.

## Frequently asked questions

### Will my baby become dependent on white noise?

A familiar sound can become one sleep cue, but “dependent” is often too dramatic. If you want more flexibility, keep other portable cues—your bedtime phrase, sleeping bag and short routine—and gradually lower or shorten the sound at an easy sleep.

### Should white noise stop when my baby falls asleep?

That is a sensible starting point. Set a short timer and see what happens. If the stopping transition repeatedly wakes your baby, try a longer timer or a gradual reduction rather than jumping straight to all-night use.

### Is pink noise better than white noise for babies?

There is no good evidence that pink noise is best for infant sleep. The 2026 REM finding came from 25 adults and should not be directly applied to babies, but it reinforces that pink noise is not automatically restorative or harmless at every dose.

### Can I run brown noise all night because it sounds softer?

Brown noise may sound deeper, not necessarily softer at the baby's ear. Use the same farther, lower, shorter approach. Sound colour does not replace checking placement, level and duration.

### What if we live beside a busy road?

Reduce environmental noise safely where practical before trying to mask it: room choice, safely closed windows and moving the cot away from a shared wall may help. If low continuous sound is still useful, keep the source distant and ask a health visitor or audiology professional if you are worried about prolonged exposure.

### Does white noise reduce SIDS risk?

No. It is not a SIDS-prevention device. Follow current safer-sleep guidance regardless of whether any sound is playing.

## The bottom line

White noise does not need to run all night simply because the button allows it. Give sound a specific job, use the lowest level that performs that job and set an end point.

If your baby notices the timer, adjust gently. If quiet works, choose quiet. If distant low sound remains useful, make that a considered choice—not a promise that one colour of noise creates better sleep.
