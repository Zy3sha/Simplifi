The first flutter can feel like bubbles, a tiny fish or a muscle twitch. Weeks later, the same baby may roll across your stomach, nudge your ribs or stretch hard enough to change the shape of your bump.

That variation is why baby movements are personal—and why one internet target cannot decide whether everything is well.

The short answer is:

- most people first feel movement between **16 and 24 weeks**
- there is **no set number** of movements that is normal for every baby
- movements usually become more noticeable up to about **32 weeks**, then stay roughly as frequent until birth, although the *type* may change
- call your midwife or maternity unit **immediately** if your baby is moving less than normal, movements feel weaker than normal, movements stop, or you feel something is wrong
- do not wait until morning, your next appointment or the end of a counting session
- do not try food, a cold drink, lying down, a home Doppler or an app before calling

Most people who are checked for one episode of reduced movement go on to have a healthy pregnancy. The check is still important because movement can be an early sign that a baby needs assessment.

## When should you first feel your baby move?

The [NHS baby-movement guide](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/your-babys-movements/) says movement is usually first felt between 16 and 24 weeks. In a first pregnancy, it may be after 20 weeks. If you have been pregnant before, you may recognise the sensation earlier.

Early movement can feel like:

- fluttering or flickering
- gentle swirling
- bubbles or tapping
- a small roll
- a light kick

It can be easy to confuse with digestion at first. There does not need to be a dramatic first kick on an exact date.

If you have **never** felt your baby move by 24 weeks, contact your midwife. They can listen to the heartbeat and arrange any further assessment that is appropriate. Do not compare your timing with somebody whose placenta, body, baby position and pregnancy history are different.

## What counts as a baby movement?

A movement does not have to be a sharp kick. Kicks, rolls, swishes, stretches, flutters and nudges all count as the baby moving.

As pregnancy progresses, the available space and the baby's position alter how movement feels. A big sweeping roll may replace some of the earlier jabs. You might feel pressure in one place and a smaller movement elsewhere. The sensation can change without the baby becoming less active.

What matters is the baby's *usual strength and frequency for this stage of pregnancy*.

Do not ignore reduced movement because somebody says the baby has “run out of room”. The Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists explains that movement frequency tends to increase until about [32 weeks and then plateau rather than decline](https://www.rcog.org.uk/for-the-public/browse-our-patient-information/your-babys-movements-in-pregnancy/). You should continue to feel movement right up to and during labour.

## How many movements are normal?

There is no universal number.

One baby may have several energetic periods each day. Another may move in smaller bursts. The placenta's position, what you are doing and which direction the baby faces can affect how easily you notice movement.

The NHS is explicit: you do not need to count kicks or movements every day, and there is no set target that proves a baby is well. Routine movement charts are not recommended for everyone because a fixed threshold can distract from the baby's individual pattern or create false reassurance.

If your own maternity team has asked you to use a particular chart or method because of your pregnancy, follow their instructions. That is an individual care plan, not a rule every pregnant person should copy.

### “Ten movements” is not an all-clear

Some counters use ten movements as a simple session target. Reaching ten can describe what you felt during that session; it cannot certify wellbeing or cancel a concern that movements are weaker, less frequent or otherwise wrong for your baby.

A baby who eventually reaches an app target after a noticeable reduction still deserves the call you were going to make. Do not wait two hours because a timer says the session is incomplete. Current 2026 information developed by Tommy's and NHS England says to [contact the maternity unit and get checked straight away](https://www.tommys.org/sites/default/files/2026-02/Reduced%20Fetal%20Movement%20A4%20DIGITAL%20-%20final%20250226.pdf) when movement is less, weaker or has stopped.

## Why movement can feel different on different days

There are ordinary reasons you may notice movements differently. They provide context, not a reason to explain away concern.

### You were busy

When walking, working or caring for another child, you may register fewer subtle movements than when you are still. That does not mean the baby necessarily moved less. But if you have already noticed a real reduction or weaker movements, do not delay the call to perform a quieter experiment.

### Your placenta is at the front

An anterior placenta can cushion movement, particularly before 28 weeks. You can still learn what is normal for *your* pregnancy. Once you have a recognisable pattern, the placenta's known position should not be used to dismiss a reduction.

### The baby changed position

Some positions make movement easier to feel in the front or sides; others make it subtler. Head-down versus bottom-first does not determine whether the baby should move.

### The baby has rest periods

Babies have periods of rest and activity. RCOG describes sleep periods that are usually short and only rarely approach 90 minutes. This is background information—not permission to watch the clock through a worrying absence of movement. You cannot know from home that a quiet period is sleep.

### Medicines, smoking or alcohol may affect movement

Some sedating medicines and substances can affect what you notice. If you are concerned, tell maternity staff what you have taken, including prescribed medicine. Do not assume the medicine explains the change and wait.

## When should you call the maternity unit?

Call straight away if:

- your baby is moving **less** than normal
- movements feel **weaker** than normal
- movements have **stopped**
- you simply feel something is wrong, even if you cannot explain it neatly

Use the contact number your maternity team gave you. Maternity units operate day and night. If you are away from home, you can contact a nearby maternity unit for direction.

Do not wait:

- until tomorrow morning
- for a routine midwife appointment
- for your partner to come home
- until you have eaten or drunk something
- for a kick-counter session to finish
- because you were assessed recently
- because it has happened before and the previous check was normal

If reduced or weaker movement happens again after a reassuring assessment, call again. You are not wasting anyone's time.

## What should you do before calling?

You do not need to complete a home test. Call first.

If possible, have your maternity notes or app contact details nearby and be ready to say:

- how many weeks pregnant you are
- when you last felt movement
- what feels less or weaker than usual
- whether movements have stopped
- whether this has happened before
- whether you have bleeding, leaking fluid, pain, contractions, severe headache, visual changes or feel unwell

You do not need a precise count to deserve assessment. “My baby is usually active after lunch and today the movements are much weaker” is useful information.

## What not to do when movement feels reduced

### Do not try to make the baby move first

Older advice often suggested a sugary snack, a cold drink or lying on the left side before seeking help. Updated 2026 patient information says **do not do anything to try to make the baby move**. These rituals can delay assessment and may produce just enough movement to create false reassurance.

Call the maternity unit and follow their instructions instead.

### Do not use a home Doppler

Hearing a heartbeat is not the same as confirming that a baby is well. It can be difficult to distinguish the baby's heartbeat from your own pulse or placental sounds, and a heartbeat does not assess the wider clinical picture.

The NHS says a home Doppler is not a reliable health check. Do not use one before calling, and do not cancel an assessment because you hear something.

### Do not use an app as a diagnostic test

A phone cannot measure fetal oxygen, growth, placental function or heart-rate patterns. It cannot know whether a tap represents a kick, a muscle twitch or a movement that feels weaker than usual.

An app can help you note what you felt. It cannot tell you to stay home.

### Do not borrow another baby's pattern

A friend who always felt forty movements before breakfast does not create your target. Nor does a quieter pregnancy make another person's reduction safe. Comparison is particularly unreliable with twins, an anterior placenta, different gestations or a first pregnancy.

## What happens when you get checked?

The exact assessment depends on gestation, your history and local pathway.

Before 24 weeks, a midwife will usually talk through movement and check the baby's heartbeat. If you have never felt movement by 24 weeks, further assessment may be arranged.

Between roughly 24 and 28 weeks, assessment can include:

- listening to the baby's heartbeat
- checking your blood pressure and urine
- measuring the size of the uterus
- reviewing risk factors and other symptoms

Later in pregnancy, maternity staff may also use cardiotocography (CTG), which records the baby's heart rate over time. An ultrasound may be arranged to assess growth, fluid or other factors when clinically indicated, particularly for persistent or recurrent reduced movements.

The purpose is not to prove you overreacted. It is to check the baby with tools that a home counter does not have.

The current [NHS England Saving Babies' Lives care bundle](https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/saving-babies-lives-version-3-2/) requires maternity providers to give movement information and follow an evidence-based pathway when reduced movement is reported. It also says people should be encouraged to return if they remain concerned.

## What if the check is reassuring?

That is good news, not embarrassment.

Most single episodes have a reassuring outcome. Ask what was checked, whether any follow-up is planned and which number to call if it happens again. You should not be sent home without understanding the result and the next step.

Then return if movements become less or weaker again—even later the same day. A recent normal CTG or ultrasound does not guarantee that a later concern should be ignored.

## Can movements increase suddenly?

Current updated messaging focuses on **less movement, weaker movement or movement stopping**. A gradual increase in strength and frequency as pregnancy progresses is expected and reassuring.

If there is a sudden burst followed by movements stopping, or you feel something is wrong, contact your maternity unit. You do not need to decide whether a dramatic episode meets a definition before asking for help.

## How to notice a pattern without becoming consumed by counting

Movement awareness should support connection, not turn pregnancy into a test you can fail.

Try this low-pressure approach when movements feel normal:

1. Notice the broad times your baby often becomes active.
2. Include rolls, swishes, stretches and flutters—not only sharp kicks.
3. Learn the usual *strength* as well as frequency.
4. Avoid demanding the same clock time or number every day.
5. Make sure you know the maternity-unit number before you need it.

You do not need to log every movement. A short note such as “usually active after dinner and at bedtime” may be more meaningful than a long total detached from context.
