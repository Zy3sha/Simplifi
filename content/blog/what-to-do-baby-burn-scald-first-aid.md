A mug tips. A small hand touches the oven door. Bath water is hotter than you expected. In the first seconds after a burn or scald, your brain may jump ahead to blame, severity and hospital. Give it one job first:

**Put the burned area under cool running water and keep it there for 20 minutes.**

Remove nearby clothing, jewellery or a nappy if it is not stuck. Keep the rest of your baby warm. After cooling, cover the area loosely with cling film or a clean, non-fluffy cloth and get medical advice.

> **Cool, call, cover: cool water for 20 minutes; call for the right help; cover only after cooling.**

This guide is for a heat burn or scald. Chemical, electrical, breathing and serious fire injuries need emergency care.

## If this has just happened: do these five things

### 1. Stop the burning

Move your baby away from the hot liquid, steam, surface or flame without putting yourself in danger. If clothing is alight, extinguish it with water or use the stop, drop and roll method.

Do not spend time photographing the injury, searching for a cream or deciding what “degree” it is. Start cooling.

### 2. Remove loose clothing, jewellery and nappies

Clothing and nappies can hold heat against the skin. Jewellery can become tight as tissue swells. Gently remove anything near the area **unless it is stuck, melted or firmly attached to the skin**.

Do not peel material away from a wound. Cool over and around it.

### 3. Cool the area under cool running water

Use a gently running cool tap or shower for **at least 20 minutes**. The NHS child-accident guidance says 20 to 30 minutes. Direct the water onto the injured area rather than placing your baby's whole body in a cold bath.

Keep cooling while somebody else calls for help if necessary. Specialist burns guidance says cooling may still help when started within three hours, so begin even if the first minutes were missed.

### 4. Keep the rest of your baby warm

Babies lose body heat quickly. While the burn is being cooled, wrap a coat or blanket around the uninjured parts of the body. Do not place fabric over the burn itself.

If your baby starts shivering, stop whole-body exposure and get urgent advice. The aim is to cool the burn, not the baby.

### 5. Cover loosely—after cooling

Lay cling film lengthways over the cooled burn. Do not wrap it tightly around a limb or joint, and do not use it on the face. A clean plastic bag can cover a hand or foot. If neither is practical, use a clean, non-fluffy cloth or non-stick dressing.

This is a temporary cover for assessment and travel, not a home dressing to leave on for days.

## The 20-minute cooling timer

Twenty minutes can feel impossibly long when your baby is upset. Use the clock rather than guessing.

| Time | Your job |
|---|---|
| 0–2 minutes | move from the heat source, remove loose clothing and start cool running water |
| 2–5 minutes | ask another adult to call 999 or NHS 111 if needed; put the phone on speaker |
| 5–10 minutes | keep the stream gentle and focused on the burn; keep the rest of baby warm |
| 10–20 minutes | continue cooling even if the skin looks calmer or your baby settles |
| after 20 minutes | cover loosely, follow the clinical advice and note what happened |

Do not interrupt cooling to apply a product. If an ambulance is coming, continue until the crew arrives unless the call handler tells you otherwise.

## Call 999 now if the injury is severe

Call 999 for a baby who is struggling to breathe, unresponsive, floppy, having a seizure, or whose skin, lips or tongue look blue, grey, very pale or blotchy. Call for a burn or scald that is very large or deep, or if the accident caused a severe injury.

The NHS also directs people to 999 or A&E for burns that:

- affect the face, genitals or bottom
- were caused by an acid or another chemical
- were caused by electricity

Do not delay cooling while waiting for the ambulance. If a facial burn followed smoke or steam exposure, breathing can worsen; tell the call handler exactly what happened.

Do not drive yourself in a life-threatening emergency. Follow the dispatcher’s instructions.

## Call NHS 111 for a baby or child burn

The British Red Cross advises seeking medical advice whenever a baby or child has been burned. The NHS says to use 111 if you are unsure what to do; for a child under five, call rather than relying on the online form.

Call NHS 111 after beginning first aid if:

- you are unsure about depth, size or location
- the skin has blistered
- pain remains significant after cooling
- the injury is on a hand, foot, joint or other functionally important area
- the burn circles a finger, arm, toe or leg
- your baby is very young
- you cannot cool or cover it safely
- the story and the skin do not seem to match

They may direct you to a GP, urgent treatment centre, minor-injuries unit, A&E or a specialist burns service. A small-looking mark on a baby's delicate skin can still need assessment.

## What not to put on a burn

Do not use:

- ice or very cold water
- butter or cooking oil
- toothpaste
- creams, gels or ointments
- flour, powder or household remedies
- adhesive plasters or fluffy cotton wool

Ice and extreme cold can damage skin further. Oils and creams can hold heat in or interfere with assessment. Sticky and fluffy dressings can attach to injured skin.

Do not burst blisters. They help protect the tissue underneath and opening them can increase infection risk.

## What if there is no running water?

Running cool tap water is best because it carries heat away continuously. If none is available, the NHS says to immerse the area in cool water or use another harmless cool fluid. Use cool bottled water if you have it.

Do not wait several minutes for a “perfect” supply while heat remains in the skin. Start with a safe cool liquid, then move to running water as soon as possible.

Never cool a baby by putting their whole body into a cold bath. Focus on the injured area and protect the rest of their temperature.

## Burn, scald, chemical or electrical injury?

The first phone call may depend on what caused it.

### A dry-heat burn

Examples include an oven door, hair straightener, iron, radiator or flame. Move away from the heat and follow cool, call, cover.

### A scald

Hot drinks, bath water, steam and cooking liquid cause scalds. Treat them with the same immediate cooling steps. Remove a wet nappy or clothing unless stuck because it can keep hot liquid against the skin.

### A chemical burn

Protect yourself from the substance. Brush off a dry powder before flooding with water and remove contaminated clothing where safe. Call 999; tell the call handler which chemical was involved and keep its container if safe to do so.

### An electrical burn

Turn off the power before touching your baby. Do not approach a live electrical source. Call 999 because the visible skin mark may not show internal injury.

## What happens after assessment?

A clinician may clean the wound, assess its depth and size, provide pain relief, apply an appropriate dressing and arrange follow-up. Burns can change appearance during the first days, which is why review matters.

Follow the dressing instructions you receive. Seek further advice if:

- pain increases or is not controlled
- the dressing becomes wet, tight, loose, smelly or soaked through
- redness or swelling spreads
- there is pus, discharge or an unpleasant smell
- your baby develops fever, becomes very sleepy, drinks less or passes less urine
- the wound is not healing as expected

Do not remove a clinical dressing simply to check the skin unless your care team told you to.

## Prevent the common repeat

Prevention is not blame. Once the immediate care is finished, change the environment that allowed the accident.

- Put hot drinks well back from the edge and never pass them over a baby.
- Avoid tablecloths and dangling placemats that can be pulled.
- Keep kettle, iron and straightener cables short and out of reach.
- Turn pan handles inward and use rear hob rings where possible.
- Check bath water before the baby enters; run cold water first, then add hot.
- Keep hot-water bottles away from babies and check manufacturer guidance.
- Remember that switched-off straighteners and hobs stay hot.
- Reassess reach whenever pulling-to-stand, cruising or climbing begins.

The Child Accident Prevention Trust warns that a hot drink can still scald a baby 15 minutes after it was made. “Warm enough for an adult to carry” does not mean safe on baby skin.

## Make first aid retrievable before you need it

Reading is useful; practising is better. Book a hands-on baby and child first-aid course through the British Red Cross, St John Ambulance, an NHS ambulance service or a reputable local provider.

Before starting solids or leaving a baby with a new carer:

1. Know where the cold tap, first-aid kit and phone are.
2. Save your local emergency and non-emergency numbers.
3. Practise holding a phone on speaker while keeping both hands available.
4. Make sure every carer knows that ice, cream and butter do not belong on burns.
5. Review choking and infant CPR with hands-on instruction.

Confidence comes from rehearsing the first action, not memorising every possible injury.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is cool water or cold water better for a burn?

Use cool running tap water. Do not use ice or extremely cold water. The aim is to remove heat from the skin without causing cold injury or chilling the baby.

### Why does cooling need 20 minutes?

Sustained cooling reduces heat in the tissue and can reduce pain, swelling and the risk of deeper injury and scarring. Keep going even if the skin begins to look calmer.

### Can I use cling film on a baby's burn?

After cooling, lay it loosely over the injury for temporary protection. Do not wrap it around a limb, do not use it on the face and do not leave it as a long-term dressing.

### Should I pop a burn blister?

No. Leave blisters intact and seek medical advice. Opening one increases the risk of infection.

### Should every baby burn be checked?

The British Red Cross advises medical advice for every baby or child burn. Call NHS 111 if you are unsure; call 999 for serious injury or emergency signs.

### Can OBubba tell me whether the burn is serious?

No. It can show a short action sequence and the correct regional phone routes, but it cannot assess the injury. Cool first and speak to a healthcare professional.
